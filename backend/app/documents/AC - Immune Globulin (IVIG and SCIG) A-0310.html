Background

Return to top of Immune Globulin (IVIG and SCIG) - AC

Immune globulin is prepared from the pooled plasma of several thousand donors to provide as complete a spectrum as possible of variable regions of antibody molecules found in the population.(1)(6)(7) (EG 2) The predominant component of immune globulin is IgG, distributed in the same proportion as in normal human serum; immune globulin also contains trace amounts of soluble CD4, HLA molecules, and cytokines.(6)(8) (EG 2) Modes of action include simple antibody replenishment as well as a variety of complex modulation processes involving several components of the immune response.(6)(7)(9) (EG 2) Several commercial preparations of immune globulin are available; however, there are insufficient data to recommend one preparation over another for use in treating a specific disease.(10) (EG 2) Immune globulin is not a generic drug, and immune globulin products should not be thought of as interchangeable; an immune globulin product needs to be selected in accordance with specific patient characteristics.(11) (EG 2) Immune globulin is available in intravenous (IVIG), subcutaneous (SCIG), and intramuscular forms.(1)(3)(12) (EG 2)

Criteria

Return to top of Immune Globulin (IVIG and SCIG) - AC

The evidence for the clinical indications found in this guideline includes 215 published peer reviewed articles, 18 specialty society or other evidence-based guidelines, 26 Cochrane systematic reviews, and 1 book section.

For acquired red cell aplasia, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) In a retrospective cohort study of 62 patients with pure red cell aplasia treated at a single institution, IVIG was associated with complete or partial response in all patients with parvovirus B19-associated pure red cell aplasia and pure red cell aplasia with hypogammaglobulinemia (4 and 11 patients treated with IVIG, respectively). IVIG was also used in 18 patients with idiopathic acquired pure red cell aplasia refractory to immunosuppressive therapy but was associated with a lower rate of response (22%).(84) (EG 2) Review articles state that IVIG is indicated for pure red cell aplasia associated with parvovirus B19 infection in immunocompromised patients on the basis of case series and case reports.(83)(85) (EG 2) An expert consensus guideline recommends IVIG for patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia who develop pure red cell aplasia associated with parvovirus B19.(86) (EG 2) A review article notes that IVIG may be an appropriate option for patients with pure red cell aplasia who fail first-line therapy (eg, prednisone, cyclophosphamide, or cyclosporine).(83) (EG 2)

For acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) An expert consensus guideline suggests that IVIG may be effective in patients not responding to corticosteroids.(30) (EG 2)

For allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) An international expert consensus guideline does not recommend routine use of IVIG in adults after hematopoietic stem cell transplant, but notes that it may be considered for patients with a serum IgG less than 400 mg/dL (4 g/L) and recurrent sinopulmonary infections and those with very low IgG levels (ie, less than 200 mg/dL (2 g/L)).(87) (EG 2) A specialty society guideline recommends against routine use of IVIG for infection prophylaxis after hematopoietic stem cell transplant but states that it may be considered for severe secondary hypogammaglobulinemia (ie, serum IgG less than 400 mg/dL (4 g/L)), particularly when associated with bacteremia or recurrent sinopulmonary infections despite antimicrobial prophylaxis.(88) (EG 2) Expert consensus guidelines recommend IVIG after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant for patients with severe hypogammaglobulinemia (ie, serum IgG less than 400 mg/dL (4 g/L)) and recurrent infections and in subsets of patients at high risk, including children undergoing transplant for disorders associated with B-cell deficiency and patients with chronic graft vs host disease with recurrent sinopulmonary infections.(89)(90) (EG 2) Another expert consensus guideline states that, while all patients undergoing allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant should be considered as potential candidates for IVIG, particular consideration should be given to patients with IgG levels below 400 mg/dL (4 g/L) and those with graft vs host disease on immunosuppressive treatment.(91) (EG 2) An expert consensus guideline on supportive care during hematopoietic stem cell transplant in pediatric patients recommends IVIG for patients with serum IgG levels below 400 mg/dL (4 g/L).(92) (EG 2)

For secondary hypogammaglobulinemia related to anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody (eg, rituximab) therapy, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) B-cell targeted therapy with monoclonal antibodies directed against CD20, particularly rituximab, is associated with a high risk of secondary hypogammaglobulinemia.(88) (EG 2) Specialty society and expert consensus guidelines recommend routine evaluation of serum immunoglobulin concentrations in rituximab-treated patients with autoimmune rheumatic diseases, with consideration for immunoglobulin replacement, particularly in patients with hypogammaglobulinemia and a history of serious, persistent, or recurrent infections.(80)(93)(94) (EG 2) Expert consensus guidelines on the management of lymphomas note that patients receiving anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody therapy are at risk for hypogammaglobulinemia and recommend consideration of IVIG in patients with frequent infections.(95)(96) (EG 2)

For catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) In an international registry study of 471 patients with catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome, patients treated with anticoagulation, corticosteroids, and either plasma exchange or IVIG had a lower mortality rate than those who did not receive any of those therapies (28.6 % vs 75%) and those who received another combination of therapies or either anticoagulation or corticosteroids alone (28.6% vs 41.1%).(99) (EG 2) Based on a review of 6 studies (343 patients) evaluating IVIG in patients with catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome that found a nonsignificantly lower mortality rate in IVIG-treated patients than non-IVIG-treated patients, an expert consensus guideline suggests the use of IVIG as first-line therapy for catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome.(97) (EG 2) Another expert consensus guideline recommends IVIG as part of combination therapy with glucocorticoids and heparin for patients with catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome.(100) (EG 2)

For autoimmune bullous diseases, including pemphigus vulgaris, pemphigus foliaceus, bullous pemphigoid, mucous membrane pemphigoid, epidermolysis bullous acquisita, pemphigoid gestationis, and linear IgA bullous dermatosis, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Expert guidelines and reviews concluded that IVIG is indicated for patients refractory to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapy.(76)(102) (EG 2) A systematic review of biologic treatment outcomes in 331 patients (154 of whom received IVIG) with mucous membrane pemphigoid found, at a median follow-up of 26.5 months, that IVIG treatment led to a complete response in 61.7% of patients, with a recurrence rate of 22.7% within 3.7 years of follow-up; the authors noted that the results were limited by the paucity of prospective studies and recommended future randomized trials.(103) (EG 1) A multicenter randomized controlled study of 56 patients with bullous pemphigoid that was resistant to prednisolone found that subsequent administration of IVIG was associated with a nonsignificant improvement, compared with placebo, in the primary endpoint of the disease activity score on day 15; the authors noted that the study may have been underpowered to detect differences in treatment groups with variable disease severity.(104) (EG 1)

For autoimmune encephalitis, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Anti-N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) encephalitis is characterized by a prodrome of headache and fever followed by sequential symptoms that may include behavioral changes, psychosis, catatonia, decreased level of consciousness, dyskinesias, and autonomic instability. Diagnosis is usually based on demonstration of NR1 IgG antibodies in serum or CSF, and initial therapy may include corticosteroids, IVIG, or plasma exchange.(109)(111)(112) (EG 2) A meta-analysis evaluating the use of immunotherapeutic agents in 1550 patients with NMDAR encephalitis (980 of whom received corticosteroids followed by IVIG as first-line therapy) found, at a median follow-up of 12 months, that use of corticosteroids plus IVIG, with or without apheresis, was associated with a good functional outcome (defined as a score of 0 to 2 on the Modified Rankin Scale).(113) (EG 1) Limbic encephalitis associated with voltage-gated potassium channel antibodies has been shown to respond within a few weeks to intense immunotherapy, which may include high-dose intravenous corticosteroids, IVIG, or plasma exchange.(109)(114) (EG 2) A review article states that IVIG can be given as first-line therapy for children with symptoms that suggest a moderate to high clinical suspicion of autoimmune encephalitis.(112) (EG 2)

For autoimmune hemolytic anemia, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Expert opinion recommends IVIG for severe life-threatening disease but not for routine use in either acute or chronic disease.(116) (EG 2) In a case series of 56 patients with severe autoimmune hemolytic anemia, IVIG was associated with a clinical response in 67% of patients.(117) (EG 2)

For chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy (eg, idecabtagene vicleucel, tisagenlecleucel)-related toxicity, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) CAR T-cell therapies directed against B-cell maturation antigen (eg, idecabtagene vicleucel) or CD19 (eg, axicabtagene ciloleucel, tisagenlecleucel) are frequently associated with B-cell aplasia and hypogammaglobulinemia, which may be prolonged.(118)(123)(124) (EG 2) An expert consensus guideline recommends IVIG for patients receiving CAR T-cell therapy with hypogammaglobulinemia (serum IgG less than 400 mg/dL (4 g/L) to 600 mg/dL (6 g/L)) who have serious or recurrent infections.(118) (EG 2) Another specialty society guideline recommends routine immunoglobulin replacement for pediatric patients receiving CAR T-cell therapy with immunoglobulin levels below age-adjusted normal ranges due to immunologic immaturity in childhood; for adults receiving CAR T-cell therapy, the authors recommend IVIG for hypogammaglobulinemia when there is a history of serious, recurrent, or chronic infections.(120) (EG 2) A specialty society guideline on the management of immune-related adverse events in patients receiving CAR T-cell therapy recommends IVIG for patients with IgG levels less than 400 mg/dL (4 g/L) and recurrent or life-threatening infections.(119) (EG 2) An expert consensus guideline notes that IVIG may also be considered for CAR T-cell therapy-related cytokine release syndrome that is refractory to corticosteroids and anti-interleukin-6 receptor antibody (anti-IL-6) therapy, but the available evidence is limited.(118) (EG 2)

For chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) Review articles and a specialty society guideline state that IVIG is an effective therapy.(128)(135)(136)(137) (EG 2) A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials comparing IVIG with other interventions for treatment of patients with definite or probable chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy identified 5 trials comparing IVIG with placebo and found, at 2 to 6 weeks of follow-up, that IVIG was associated with improvement in disability compared with placebo, with persistent benefit seen up to 24 weeks of follow-up. Two trials comparing IVIG with corticosteroids (prednisolone or methylprednisolone) found, at 2 weeks of follow-up, no difference in disability improvement between groups.(133) (EG 1) A randomized phase III trial of 172 adult patients with chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy who had previously responded to IVIG found that administration of low-dose or high-dose weekly SCIG for 24 weeks, compared with placebo, was effective at maintaining significant freedom from relapse in both active treatment groups; relapse rates were 63%, 39%, and 33%, respectively, in the placebo, low-dose active treatment, and high-dose active treatment groups, with low rates of adverse effects. The authors noted that this study did not directly compare IVIG with SCIG; in addition, data were missing on patients who withdrew from the study.(131) (EG 1) In a multicenter single-arm open-label trial, 49 patients with chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (typical or atypical) were treated with induction IVIG for 5 days followed by maintenance IVIG every 3 weeks; patients with a response at week 28 continued maintenance for a total of 52 weeks. At week 28, the responder rate was 77.6%; of the 34 patients who were enrolled in maintenance through the conclusion of the trial, 69.4% maintained improvement and 10.5% had a relapse.(138) (EG 2) An expert consensus guideline recommends IVIG for the treatment of chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy.(4) (EG 2)

For dermatomyositis or polymyositis, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) Expert guidelines and review articles recommend IVIG for patients refractory to immunosuppressive therapy.(30)(76)(141)(142)(143) (EG 2) A systematic review found one randomized placebo-controlled trial, which revealed that treatment with IVIG produced statistically significant improvement in muscle strength over a course of 3 months.(140) (EG 1) A randomized controlled trial of 95 patients with active dermatomyositis compared treatment with either IVIG or placebo and found, at 16-week follow-up, that IVIG was associated with greater rates of disease improvement (defined as a composite Total Improvement Score of at least 20 and no deterioration while receiving treatment) compared with placebo; the authors recommended larger studies with longer follow-up to fully assess long-term efficacy and safety.(144) (EG 1) A review article found only 2 additional randomized controlled studies supporting the efficacy of IVIG in relapsed, refractory, or steroid-dependent disease, or as part of first-line therapy in older patients; the authors stressed the need for more robust evidence, using validated outcome measures to guide therapy decisions.(145) (EG 1) An expert consensus guideline recommends IVIG for the treatment of dermatomyositis but found insufficient evidence to recommend its use for polymyositis.(4) (EG 2)

For fetal-neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) A systematic review reported that IVIG, with or without corticosteroids, may be used as first-line therapy of fetal-neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia; however, the optimal timing and dosing have not been established.(149) (EG 1) A retrospective case series of 22 neonates suggests that antenatal weekly maternal IVIG is effective and safe for the treatment of fetal-neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia.(146) (EG 2) An expert consensus guideline considers IVIG to be standard first-line antenatal therapy as well as appropriate adjunctive therapy of a newborn, after first-line therapy with antigen-negative compatible platelets.(147) (EG 2)

For Guillain-Barre syndrome, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) A systematic review and meta-analysis found comparable efficacy and safety for plasma exchange and IVIG for the management of Guillain-Barre syndrome.(153) (EG 1) A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials has confirmed effectiveness of IVIG as first-line therapy comparable to that of therapeutic plasma exchange when started within 2 weeks of onset of severe disease.(154) (EG 1) A randomized controlled trial of 93 patients with Guillain-Barre syndrome with poor prognosis (defined as a score of 6 or greater on the modified Erasmus Guillain-Barre Syndrome Outcome Score) treated with an initial dose of IVIG followed by either a second IVIG dose or placebo found, at 4-week follow-up, that a second IVIG dose was not associated with improved Guillain-Barre syndrome disability scores and resulted in more serious adverse events compared with placebo.(155) (EG 1) An expert consensus guideline on the diagnosis and treatment of Guillain-Barre syndrome in children and adolescents recommends IVIG for patients with severe or progressive disease.(156) (EG 2) Expert consensus guidelines recommend IVIG for the treatment of Guillain-Barre syndrome in adult and pediatric patients.(4)(152) (EG 2)

For hematologic malignancies, such as chronic lymphocytic leukemia or multiple myeloma, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Expert consensus guidelines recommend the use of IVIG for selected patients with recurrent or severe infections and low serum IgG levels, as well as for patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia and corticosteroid-refractory autoimmune hemolytic anemia.(86)(158)(159) (EG 2) IVIG is recommended for patients with acute lymphoblastic leukemia who have serum IgG levels less than 400 mg/dL (4 g/L) and are considered at high risk for chemotherapy-related morbidity and mortality, particularly infants and patients with Down syndrome, or are undergoing CD19-targeted chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy.(160)(161) (EG 2) An expert consensus guideline recommends that immunoglobulin replacement therapy be considered for patients with hematologic malignancies who have severe, recurrent, or persistent infections despite anti-infective therapy and have IgG levels less than 400 mg/dL (4 g/L) or fail a test immunization.(91) (EG 2) A systematic review of 21 randomized controlled trials evaluating interventions to reduce infections in patients with hematologic malignancies, including 8 studies comparing prophylactic immunoglobulin with placebo or no intervention and one study comparing immunoglobulin with antibiotics, found that prophylactic immunoglobulin was associated with a reduced risk of clinically documented infection, as compared with placebo or no intervention, but not associated with reduced risk of all-cause or infection-related mortality.(162) (EG 1)

For hemolytic disease of the newborn, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) A systematic review and meta-analysis found that those studies suggesting benefit of IVIG for Rh hemolytic disease had the highest risks of bias, and efficacy of IVIG for treatment of ABO hemolytic disease was not clear for the same reason.(168) (EG 1) A systematic review and meta-analysis of 9 studies with 658 infants found that, while IVIG administration was associated with significantly reduced need for exchange transfusion, the quality of the evidence in general was low to very low, and the 2 studies with the lowest risk of bias showed no benefit of IVIG in reducing the need for or number of exchange transfusions.(170) (EG 1) Long-term follow-up of 66 patients with hemolytic disease of the newborn found that, after 2 to 7 years, IVIG administration, as compared with placebo, was associated with no significant difference in neurodevelopmental outcomes; the authors recommended further long-term studies.(169) (EG 2) A specialty society guideline recommends administration of IVIG in infants with isoimmune hemolytic disease and rising total serum bilirubin level.(167) (EG 2)

For hemolytic transfusion reaction, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) On the basis of case reports, review articles suggest the use of IVIG for acute hemolytic transfusion reactions, particularly for severe disease.(171)(172) (EG 2) A specialty society guideline on transfusion support for sickle cell disease suggests immunosuppressive therapy, including IVIG, for treatment of severe hemolytic transfusion reactions with hyperhemolysis.(173) (EG 2)

For hemophagocytic syndrome, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) A systematic review and meta-analysis of 36 studies (493 patients) evaluating treatment of hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis in children (mean age 49.5 months) in the intensive care unit (161 of whom received IVIG) found that IVIG was associated with decreased mortality; the authors noted that the retrospective nature of some of the included studies limited the results, and further large prospective studies were recommended.(178) (EG 1) A specialty society guideline on the management of hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis in adults recommends the use of IVIG for clinically unstable or worsening patients with hemophagocytic syndrome secondary to a variety of causes (eg, infection, malignancy).(175) (EG 2) Another specialty society guideline recommends IVIG as a treatment option for suspected hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis in adults when there is persistent, severe, or worsening inflammation or organ dysfunction.(177) (EG 2)

For HIV-associated hematologic complications, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) A systematic review suggests that IVIG is effective for rapidly increasing platelet count in patients who are actively bleeding.(179) (EG 1) For HIV-infected children, a systematic review and a practice guideline recommend IVIG for prevention of bacterial infection in children with coexistent hypogammaglobulinemia.(179)(180) (EG 2)

For idiopathic (immune) thrombocytopenic purpura, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) A systematic review of randomized controlled trials reported that low-dose IVIG is effective in rapidly increasing the platelet count to prevent or control bleeding.(186) (EG 1) A systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 randomized trials including 558 children with immune thrombocytopenia compared treatment with IVIG or anti-D immunoglobulins and found, at 24 to 72 hours after administration, that IVIG was associated with more patients achieving a platelet count greater than 20,000/mm3 (20 x109/L) compared with anti-D immunoglobulins.(187) (EG 1) A randomized trial of 206 pediatric patients (age 3 months to 16 years) with newly diagnosed idiopathic immune thrombocytopenia compared treatment with a single infusion of IVIG or observation and found, at 3-month follow-up, that IVIG was associated with more patients achieving a complete response (defined as a platelet count greater than 100,000/mm3 (100 x109/L)) compared with observation; no difference in the development of chronic idiopathic immune thrombocytopenia or complete response rates at 6-month or 12-month follow-up was seen between the groups.(188) (EG 1) An expert consensus guideline recommends IVIG combined with corticosteroids in patients with idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura when a rapid rise in platelets is warranted and suggests either IVIG or anti-D immunoglobulin in patients who require treatment and have contraindications to corticosteroids.(182) (EG 2) Another expert consensus guideline recommends IVIG as first-line treatment for children with acute immune thrombocytopenic purpura who require treatment (eg, for severe spontaneous mucosal bleeding).(189) (EG 2)

For immune checkpoint inhibitor-related toxicity, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Immune checkpoint inhibitors (eg, pembrolizumab, atezolizumab, nivolumab, ipilimumab) have been associated with a range of early-onset and late-onset inflammatory and autoimmune reactions that have typically been managed with immunosuppressive therapy.(118)(192) (EG 2) An expert consensus guideline recommends IVIG for management of immune checkpoint inhibitor-related myocarditis, demyelinating disease, and pneumonitis that is refractory to high-dose corticosteroids; severe immune checkpoint inhibitor-related bullous dermatitis and Stevens-Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis, myasthenia gravis, and Guillain-Barre syndrome; immune checkpoint inhibitor-related myositis that is severe, associated with dysphagia, or refractory to high-dose corticosteroids; and severe or rapidly progressing immune checkpoint inhibitor-related encephalitis.(118) (EG 2) A specialty society guideline on the management of immunotherapy-related toxicities recommends IVIG for grade 3 and above immune-related interstitial lung disease and corticosteroid-refractory pneumonitis, grade 3 and above immune-related myositis, and grade 3 and above immune-related neurologic symptoms.(192) (EG 2) Another specialty society guideline on immune checkpoint inhibitor-related adverse events recommends IVIG for grade 3 and above bullous dermatoses, grade 4 severe cutaneous adverse reactions, grade 3 and above corticosteroid-refractory pneumonitis, grade 3 and above myositis, grade 3 and above myasthenia gravis, Guillain-Barre syndrome of any severity, severe or progressing encephalitis, grade 3 and above demyelinating disease, grade 4 hemolytic anemia, and immune thrombocytopenic purpura, particularly when a rapid rise in platelets is needed.(190) (EG 2)

For infection prophylaxis, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Expert consensus guidelines indicate that intramuscular immune globulin is indicated after hepatitis A exposure in individuals in whom hepatitis A vaccination is contraindicated, immunocompromised patients, and those age 40 years or older or with chronic liver disease. Intramuscular immune globulin may also be indicated for individuals traveling to areas of high or intermediate hepatitis A endemicity for those age younger than 6 months or 40 years or older, who are unvaccinated, are immunocompromised, or with chronic liver disease.(198) (EG 2) Expert consensus guidelines recommend immune globulin for individuals at risk of measles (unvaccinated and no prior measles disease) after a known measles exposure.(199) (EG 2) Risk of rubella infection in nonimmune individuals may be reduced by immune globulin administration within 5 days of exposure.(201) (EG 1) Although there is limited evidence about the effectiveness of immune globulin for prevention of congenital rubella syndrome, expert opinion suggests that intramuscular immune globulin may be considered for pregnant patients who are nonimmune to rubella after a known rubella exposure.(200) (EG 2) For prevention of varicella infection, the use of varicella zoster-specific immune globulin is recommended for those without evidence of immunity and at high risk for complications (eg, immunocompromised or pregnant patients). However, IVIG may be administered when varicella zoster-specific immune globulin is unavailable.(202)(203) (EG 2)

For Kawasaki disease, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) IVIG is effective in reducing the incidence of coronary artery aneurysms as well as reducing fever and other clinical signs and symptoms.(211)(212)(214)(215)(216) (EG 2) A systematic review of 31 randomized controlled trials evaluating the efficacy of IVIG for the treatment of Kawasaki disease included 11 studies (1437 patients) comparing IVIG with aspirin and found moderate-certainty evidence that IVIG was associated with shorter fever duration and reduced incidence of coronary artery abnormalities at 30-day follow-up compared with aspirin.(217) (EG 1) An open-label randomized trial of 103 patients (age 4 weeks to 17 years) with Kawasaki disease resistant to IVIG (continued fever at least 36 hours after initial IVIG dose) compared treatment with either a second dose of IVIG or infliximab and found that infliximab was associated with more patients achieving resolution of fever, fewer total days of fever, and shorter hospital stay compared with IVIG, with no difference in coronary artery outcomes seen between groups. The authors recommended larger studies to further evaluate efficacy for coronary artery outcomes.(218) (EG 1)

For kidney transplant, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) An expert guideline recommends preoperative and perioperative IVIG for recipients with anti-HLA antibody sensitization to living donor kidneys.(72) (EG 2) A subsequent matched case control study demonstrated that preoperative treatment with IVIG and plasmapheresis provided a significant survival benefit for kidney transplant recipients with HLA antibody sensitization to living donor kidneys, as compared with patients on a transplant waiting list who had similar levels of panel-reactive antibodies.(219) (EG 2) For antibody-mediated rejection of a kidney transplant from either a deceased or a live donor, an expert consensus guideline recommends that IVIG be administered after each plasmapheresis treatment.(72) (EG 2)

For Lambert-Eaton syndrome, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) A systematic review found only one small randomized crossover trial, which demonstrated effectiveness of IVIG in improving muscle strength for severe cases when steroids and other immunosuppressive treatments did not control symptoms; however, improvement lasted only for a period of several weeks.(226) (EG 1) A systematic review and meta-analysis of therapies for paraneoplastic neuropathies, including Lambert-Eaton syndrome, found no randomized controlled trials and only 6 studies involving 54 patients that suggested that IVIG is helpful.(227) (EG 1) An expert consensus guideline recommends IVIG for the treatment of Lambert-Eaton syndrome.(4) (EG 2)

For multifocal motor neuropathy, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) A systematic review of 6 randomized trials (90 patients) evaluating the efficacy of immunoglobulin for the treatment of multifocal motor neuropathy found that IVIG treatment improved muscle strength and may improve disability; the authors noted that the small number of included patients limited the conclusions regarding disability improvement, and further randomized trials were recommended.(230) (EG 1) An expert consensus guideline recommends IVIG for the treatment of multifocal motor neuropathy.(4) (EG 2)

For multiple sclerosis, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) A specialty society guideline suggests that IVIG may have utility in the second-line or third-line treatment setting for relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis in patients who do not tolerate or have contraindications to first-line therapies.(30) (EG 1) Evidence for effectiveness of IVIG for this condition is strongest in studies of the adult population; studies in the pediatric population are limited to a small number of case reports, with no reports as yet on the use of IVIG as a first-line therapy in pediatric multiple sclerosis.(231) (EG 2)

For multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C) in the setting of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) infection, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) A multicenter, open-label, randomized controlled trial using an adaptive platform design randomized 214 inpatients with MIS-C to treatment with IVIG, methylprednisolone, or usual care and found that IVIG was not associated with an improved mean duration of hospital stay as compared with usual care (7.4 days in the IVIG group vs 7.6 days in the usual-care group). However, the authors noted that a high proportion of patients randomized to usual care received an off-protocol study treatment, including IVIG in 35% of patients assigned to usual care in the IVIG vs usual care comparison, which may have reduced the trial's ability to detect a treatment effect.(238) (EG 1) A multicenter, open-label, randomized controlled trial compared a 3-day course of intravenous methylprednisolone with a single dose of IVIG in 75 patients with MIS-C and found no between-group differences in the median hospital length of stay (6 days in the methylprednisolone group vs 6 days in the IVIG group). However, patients in the methylprednisolone group were less likely to require respiratory support than those in the IVIG group (27% vs 55%).(239) (EG 1) A multisite observational study of 518 patients (median age 8.7 years) with MIS-C evaluated the efficacy of immunomodulatory therapy (103 propensity score-matched patients received IVIG plus glucocorticoids and 103 received IVIG alone) and found that IVIG plus glucocorticoids was associated with lower risk of cardiovascular dysfunction (left ventricular dysfunction or shock resulting in use of vasopressors) on or after day 2 of follow-up compared with IVIG alone.(240) (EG 2) An international observational study of 614 children with suspected MIS-C evaluated the efficacy of immunomodulatory therapy (246 received IVIG, 208 received IVIG plus glucocorticoids, and 90 received glucocorticoids alone) and found no difference between the 3 groups in disease severity reduction measured at day 2; the authors noted, however, that the conclusions were limited by the lack of randomization and the rapidly changing treatment environment, and they recommend larger studies with longer follow-up.(241) (EG 2) Expert consensus guidelines recommend IVIG as first-line treatment for MIS-C, in conjunction with corticosteroids with or without anakinra for patients with severe or refractory disease.(233)(234)(235)(236)(242) (EG 2)

For myasthenia gravis, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) A systematic review and a subsequent study concluded that IVIG may have effectiveness comparable to plasma exchange for temporary symptomatic improvement in severe exacerbations or acute crises.(244)(246)(247) (EG 1) A review article concluded that IVIG and plasma exchange are indeed considered equally effective in treating severe cases of myasthenia gravis.(243) (EG 2) A randomized controlled trial of 60 patients with generalized myasthenia gravis (all of whom were dependent on corticosteroids) compared treatment with either IVIG (loading dose followed by maintenance doses) or placebo and found, at 39-week follow-up, no difference between groups in the proportion of patients achieving a 50% or greater reduction in their corticosteroid dose.(248) (EG 1) A retrospective chart review of 52 patients treated with IVIG for myasthenia gravis found that 23 and 14 patients, respectively, achieved mild and moderate symptomatic improvement over an average of 5.9 years, but none achieved complete remission.(249) (EG 2) Guidelines from an international task force on myasthenia gravis conclude that IVIG is appropriate for short-term treatment for managing acute severe symptoms, in preparation for surgery, when rapid response to treatment is needed, when other therapies are ineffective, and before starting corticosteroids to minimize exacerbations. The authors note that chronic IVIG may be considered for patients with refractory myasthenia gravis.(245) (EG 2) An expert consensus guideline concludes that IVIG is effective for treating exacerbations of myasthenia gravis but does not recommend IVIG for the treatment of chronic stable disease.(4) (EG 2) Another expert consensus guideline recommends IVIG as an option for treating symptoms in active myasthenia gravis that is refractory to other therapies and for therapy in a myasthenic crisis. The authors recommend that IVIG be considered as maintenance therapy on an individualized basis, particularly in patients with a relative contraindication to other immunosuppressants and in patients with juvenile myasthenia gravis.(225) (EG 2)

For opsoclonus-myoclonus syndrome, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) An open-label randomized trial of 53 patients (all age 8 years or younger) with neuroblastoma or ganglioneuroblastoma associated with opsoclonus-myoclonus syndrome compared treatment with prednisone combined with either cyclophosphamide or risk-adapted therapy with and without concurrent IVIG and found, at a median follow-up of 143 days, that IVIG was associated with greater improvement or normalization of opsoclonus-myoclonus-related symptoms after 1 year (81% and 41% in the IVIG and no-IVIG groups, respectively).(251) (EG 1) Expert opinion suggests that IVIG is a treatment option for opsoclonus-myoclonus syndrome secondary to both neoplastic and non-neoplastic causes.(253)(254) (EG 2)

For post-transfusion purpura, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Expert opinion suggests that IVIG is standard therapy for post-transfusion purpura.(172)(255) (EG 2)

For pregnancy-associated idiopathic (immune) thrombocytopenic purpura, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Specialty society guidelines recommend IVIG for treatment of immune thrombocytopenia during pregnancy.(256)(257)(258) (EG 2)

For primary immunodeficiency, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) A specialty society guideline and reviews recommend IVIG as replacement therapy for patients with primary immunodeficiencies.(266)(269)(270)(271) (EG 2) A meta-analysis concluded that keeping trough IVIG levels at or above 1000 mg/dL (10 g/L) is likely associated with a significantly lower risk of pneumonia.(272) (EG 1) An observational study found that IVIG infusion resulted in significantly protective levels of various common antipneumococcal antibodies in patients with primary immunodeficiency.(273) (EG 2) A technology assessment found 16 studies suggesting that subcutaneous infusion of immunoglobulin for primary and secondary immunodeficiency may be as safe and effective as intravenous infusion of immunoglobulin, but the quality of the available evidence is low, significantly reducing the certainty of such findings.(274) (EG 1) For patients with specific antibody deficiency (ie, patients with recurrent respiratory tract infections, normal immunoglobulin and IgG subclass levels, and impaired response to pneumococcal capsular polysaccharide), a specialty society guideline notes that IgG replacement with IVIG may be indicated when other therapies (eg, repeat immunization with conjugate pneumococcal vaccine, antibiotics) have been ineffective.(266) (EG 2)

For Rasmussen encephalitis, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Review articles indicate, on the basis of case reports and case series, that IVIG may be useful for short-term therapy for Rasmussen encephalitis, a rare disorder characterized by focal epilepsy, lateralized brain atrophy, and cognitive impairment.(112)(277)(279) (EG 2) Smaller studies that are inconclusive suggest that IVIG may be effective as chronic therapy in patients who have not yet developed refractory epilepsy, with the caveat that the disease process may become arrested and prevent patients from reaching the point where they require surgery.(278) (EG 2)

For Stevens-Johnson syndrome or toxic epidermal necrolysis, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) A systematic review of 67 studies evaluating immunomodulating therapies for Stevens-Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis overlap or toxic epidermal necrolysis identified 38 studies evaluating IVIG alone or in combination with other therapies (5 prospective comparative studies, 33 retrospective comparative studies or case series). In a network meta-analysis of 11 treatment arms, combination therapy with IVIG and corticosteroids was associated with improved mortality rates compared with mortality rates predicted by a toxic epidermal necrolysis-specific severity-of-illness score.(282) (EG 1) A meta-analysis of 13 observational studies found that higher dosages of IVIG correlated strongly with decreased mortality in patients with Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis.(283) (EG 1) Expert consensus guidelines state that IVIG is an appropriate therapy for Stevens-Johnson syndrome or toxic epidermal necrolysis.(76)(102)(281) (EG 2)

For stiff-person syndrome, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Based on the results of a small randomized controlled trial, expert guidelines recommend IVIG for those failing first-line therapy with GABA agonist medications, such as baclofen or benzodiazepines.(30)(284) (EG 2) An expert consensus guideline concludes that IVIG is effective for treating stiff-person syndrome.(4) (EG 2)

For systemic lupus erythematosus, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Expert consensus guidelines and reviews suggest that IVIG may be appropriate in severely ill patients not responding to standard therapy,(76)(102)(286)(287) especially in those with concomitant infections.(285) (EG 2) A systematic review and meta-analysis found that the use of IVIG seems to be associated with significant reduction in disease activity and increase in complement levels, but the limited number of trials prevented assessment of other clinical outcome measures such as anti-double-stranded DNA, antinuclear antibody, steroid dosage, and renal function.(288) (EG 1) A retrospective case series describes complete remission after IVIG administration in 9 of 27 patients with active disease and concomitant infection, and in 6 of 26 patients with active disease resistant to standard therapy.(289) (EG 2) A specialty society guideline recommends IVIG as part of the treatment of severe autoimmune thrombocytopenia and catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus.(290) (EG 2)

For acute viral myocarditis, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A systematic review and meta-analysis identified 2 adult trials and one pediatric trial evaluating IVIG in acute presumed viral myocarditis. One placebo-controlled trial evaluated 62 adults with recent-onset dilated cardiomyopathy and found no significant difference between groups in terms of improvement in left ventricular ejection fraction or functional capacity. The second randomized trial of 41 adults with acute myocarditis without evidence of valvular or ischemic heart disease compared high-dose IVIG with no treatment and reported longer survival time in the IVIG-treated patients (at an average follow-up of 60 days); however, risk of bias and uncertain evidence quality limited the conclusions. A pediatric randomized trial evaluated the addition of IVIG to conventional therapy in 86 children with acute-onset dilated cardiomyopathy and found no difference between groups in overall survival, left ventricular end-diastolic diameter, or left ventricular shortening fraction at 6-month follow-up. The systematic review authors concluded that the evidence for IVIG for presumed viral myocarditis is uncertain and recommended additional higher-quality studies with low risk of bias and larger sample sizes.(13) (EG 1) A multi-institutional retrospective review of 216 pediatric patients with acute myocarditis found no evidence supporting the efficacy of IVIG in terms of improvement in cardiac function or survival; it stated that randomized controlled trials should be performed to determine if IVIG is appropriate for this condition.(14) (EG 2)

For Alzheimer disease, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A multicenter, randomized, placebo-controlled, phase II trial of 58 patients with mild to moderate Alzheimer disease found an effective safety and tolerability profile for IVIG, but this and other review articles cautioned that longer studies with more patients are required to assess possible effects on cognition and function.(15)(16)(17) (EG 1) A phase III double-blind placebo-controlled trial of 390 patients with mild to moderate Alzheimer disease found that biweekly administration of IVIG at 2 different dosages for 18 months, compared with placebo, resulted in no significant improvement in the outcome measures of cognition or improvement in activities of daily living; treatment with IVIG was associated with adverse effects including chills and rashes.(18) (EG 1)

For amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) An expert guideline states that there is insufficient evidence to recommend treatment with IVIG for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.(19) (EG 2)

For atopic dermatitis, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A systematic review evaluating the efficacy of systemic treatments for atopic dermatitis included 3 studies (64 patients) of IVIG. One study in children reported improvements in atopic dermatitis severity with IVIG; other studies found no improvement with IVIG compared with placebo in adults or cyclosporine in children. The authors noted that all trials had high risk of bias and were limited by unclear reporting on patient age.(20) (EG 1)

For chronic fatigue syndrome, evidence demonstrates a lack of net benefit; additional research is recommended. (RG C1) A systematic review of treatments for adults with myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome found a single randomized controlled trial evaluating IVIG and concluded that there was no benefit from this therapy.(21) (EG 1) An expert guideline concluded that IVIG is unlikely to be beneficial and is not recommended for treatment of chronic fatigue syndrome.(22) (EG 2)

For complex regional pain syndrome, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A multicenter randomized placebo-controlled trial with 111 patients with moderate or severe complex regional pain syndrome and disease of 1 to 5 years' duration found no significant improvement in 24-hour average pain intensity or quality of life associated with 6 weeks of treatment with low-dose IVIG compared with placebo.(23) (EG 1)

For critical illness polyneuropathy, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A randomized controlled trial of 38 critically ill patients with multiple organ failure, systemic inflammatory response syndrome, and clinical signs of critical illness polyneuropathy with or without myopathy compared treatment with either IgM-enriched IVIG or placebo and found, at 14-day follow-up, no difference in symptoms severity scores between groups. The study was terminated early due to futility.(24) (EG 1) A review of neuromuscular complications of critical illness states that IVIG has not shown improvement in critical illness polyneuropathy.(25) (EG 2)

For dilated cardiomyopathy, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A pilot study of 17 patients with endomyocardial biopsy-proven high viral load of Human parvovirus B19 found that administration of high-dose IVIG was associated with significant improvement in viral load, ejection fraction, and New York Heart Association functional class over a period of 12 months. However, the authors indicated that randomized placebo-controlled trials are needed.(26) (EG 2) A randomized controlled trial of 50 patients with chronic idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy (all of whom were treated with standard medical therapy and had Human parvovirus B19 DNA levels of greater than 200 copies/mcg on endomyocardial biopsy) compared treatment with either IVIG or placebo and found, at 6-month follow-up, that IVIG did not improve left ventricular ejection fraction or 6-minute walk test times compared with placebo.(27) (EG 1) In a randomized controlled study of 62 patients with acute myocardial infarction and decreased ejection fraction, administration of monthly IVIG for 6 months did not result in any significant improvement in either left ventricular remodeling or function.(28) (EG 1)

For epilepsy, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A systematic review found insufficient evidence to draw conclusions about the effectiveness of IVIG for epilepsy, and the authors stated that additional randomized controlled trials are needed.(29) (EG 1) An expert consensus guideline notes that IVIG seems to have a favorable effect in refractory epilepsy; however, this conclusion is based on limited evidence from case reports and small case series.(30) (EG 2)

For Graves ophthalmopathy, evidence demonstrates a lack of net benefit; additional research is recommended. (RG C1) A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials found studies showing no incremental benefit of IVIG over standard therapy with oral corticosteroids and concluded that IVIG has marginal or unproven efficacy for this condition.(31) (EG 1)

For hematopoietic stem cell transplant without hypogammaglobulinemia, evidence demonstrates a lack of net benefit; additional research is recommended. (RG C1) A systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that the use of IVIG as routine infection prophylaxis did not prevent infection or prolong survival.(32) (EG 1)

For IgG subclass deficiency with normal total IgG levels, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) An uncontrolled study of 10 patients with IgG subclass deficiency receiving monthly IVIG for 12 months reported significant improvement in infection rate, antibiotic use, and quality of life during IVIG administration. The authors indicated that larger randomized controlled studies will be required for confirmation.(33) (EG 2) A retrospective study of 13 patients treated with IVIG reported that 11 patients had improvement in the number of infections per year and 2 patients had no improvement. The authors stated that there was no correlation between response to IVIG and immunologic parameters, and the sample size was too small to reach a definitive conclusion.(34) (EG 2) An expert consensus guideline concluded that there is insufficient evidence to support adding IgG subclass deficiency or isolated IgA deficiency to the recommended indications for IVIG therapy.(35) (EG 2)

For inclusion body myositis, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) Expert guidelines state that there is insufficient evidence to support the use of IVIG for inclusion body myositis.(4)(30) (EG 2) A systematic review on interventions for dysphagia in long-term progressive muscle disease found a single randomized study involving patients with inclusion body myositis; because the study had a high risk of bias and did not specify some key outcomes, the authors concluded that there was insufficient evidence to determine if this intervention was efficacious for inclusion body myositis.(36) (EG 1)

For nephrotic syndrome, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A systematic review identified 4 studies that showed a beneficial effect of IVIG on the prevention of nosocomial infections in children with nephrotic syndrome; however, it was concluded that there is insufficient evidence to support the use of IVIG because the methodological quality of all studies was poor, the sample sizes were small, and all studies were from China.(37) (EG 2)

For neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A review article and a case series indicated that case-level evidence supports the use of IVIG in relapse treatment or prevention, especially in patients who cannot tolerate corticosteroids, but larger randomized trials are needed for confirmation.(16)(38)(39) (EG 2) A multicenter randomized controlled study initially targeting a total of 170 adults or children with acute first presentation of either transverse myelitis or neuromyelitis optica was to assess the effectiveness of IVIG in addition to methylprednisolone alone during the first 5 days of steroid therapy, but the study was only able to recruit a total of 26 patients for screening, of which only 2 were randomized into the study. Consequently, there could be no determination of effectiveness of IVIG for these related conditions.(40) (EG 1) In some cases, optic neuritis may be a presentation of myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease (MOG-AD).(41)(42) (EG 2) A systematic review and network meta-analysis of 12 studies including 735 patients with MOG-AD (34 of whom received IVIG treatment) found that IVIG decreased the incidence of relapse compared with no treatment and that IVIG was the most effective of 10 treatments studied for reducing incidence of relapse. However, the authors noted that the retrospective nature of the included studies and the lack of standard dosing regimens limited the results, and larger prospective studies with standard regimens were recommended to verify the efficacy of IVIG.(43) (EG 1) Review articles note that IVIG has been used in case series and individual patients to treat neuromyelitis optica associated with myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibodies, but evidence for different treatment approaches is limited.(44)(45)(46)(47)(48) (EG 2)

For paraproteinemic demyelinating neuropathy, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A systematic review reported that there was inadequate evidence to support any immunomodulatory therapy, including IVIG, for this condition.(49) (EG 2) Expert guidelines have concluded that there is insufficient evidence to support the effectiveness of IVIG for this condition.(4)(50) (EG 2)

For pediatric autoimmune neuropsychiatric disorders associated with streptococcal infections (PANDAS), evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) Reviews of the medical literature have identified reports of IVIG having beneficial effects on tics and/or obsessive-compulsive disorder but have concluded that immunomodulator therapy such as IVIG has potential serious adverse effects and requires further research before it can be recommended.(51)(52) (EG 2)

For postpolio syndrome, evidence demonstrates a lack of net benefit; additional research is recommended. (RG C1) A systematic review concluded that there is moderate-quality evidence that IVIG has no beneficial effect on activity limitations and that the evidence is inconsistent for effectiveness of IVIG on muscle strength and pain.(53) (EG 1) A randomized trial of 51 patients treated with either IVIG or placebo found that some components of health-related quality of life improved after 2 months, although gait, muscle strength, fatigue, and bodily pain did not change; overall, the primary endpoint was not met, and the authors indicated that further randomized studies are needed.(54) (EG 1) Expert guidelines have concluded that evidence is insufficient to support the use of IVIG for postpolio syndrome.(4)(30) (EG 2)

For the prevention of complete heart block (congenital), evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) Two open-label observational studies failed to demonstrate effectiveness of IVIG to prevent this condition in high-risk women who had previously delivered a child with complete heart block.(55)(56) (EG 2) A specialty society guideline on the diagnosis and treatment of fetal cardiac disease recommends in utero IVIG for the management of known fetal atrioventricular block but states that IVIG is not recommended for the prevention of heart block.(57) (EG 2)

For recurrent pregnancy loss or infertility, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating the efficacy of immunotherapy on pregnancy outcomes in women with abnormal levels of natural killer cells and recurrent miscarriage or implantation failure included 4 nonrandomized trials of IVIG (557 patients) and found that treatment with IVIG improved live birth rates compared with control. However, the authors noted the results were limited by significant heterogeneity and lack of a standard dosing protocol, and further randomized studies to assess efficacy and safety were recommended.(58) (EG 1) A systematic review and meta-analysis identified 6 randomized trials studying the use of IVIG to improve the probability of a live birth in women with unexplained recurrent miscarriage; the authors found no benefit of IVIG for this indication and stated that it should not be used outside of randomized controlled trials.(59) (EG 1) A single-center, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 82 women with unexplained secondary recurrent miscarriages found no evidence that IVIG significantly increased the live birth rate, and the authors stated that it cannot be recommended in clinical practice.(60) (EG 1) A systematic review and meta-analysis identified 10 studies of patients with repeated in vitro fertilization failure and found that IVIG administration was associated with significantly improved implantation and clinical pregnancy rates, as well as a reduced rate of miscarriage; however, the incremental improvement in live birth rate per embryo transferred was not clinically significant, and the authors indicated that additional randomized controlled trials are needed.(61) (EG 1)

For rheumatic fever, evidence demonstrates a lack of net benefit; additional research is recommended. (RG C1) A systematic review identified one study of the effect of using IVIG vs placebo to prevent cardiac disease in patients with acute rheumatic fever; the benefit was not statistically significant, and it was concluded that there is no evidence to support the use of IVIG to reduce the risk of developing heart valve lesions.(62) (EG 1)

For rheumatoid arthritis, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A review article states that while IVIG is used as an off-label treatment for various autoimmune diseases, effectiveness has not yet been confirmed for rheumatoid arthritis; several case series suggest, but do not confirm, efficacy in patients for whom standard therapies are contraindicated or intolerable, including those with Felty syndrome, Still disease, pregnancy with active disease, or infection in the setting of active disease.(63) (EG 2)

For sepsis or septic shock in adults, evidence demonstrates a lack of net benefit; additional research is recommended. (RG C1) A systematic review and meta-analysis and a review article found some evidence that polyclonal IVIG reduced mortality in adults with sepsis, but this benefit was not observed in trials with lower risks of bias; for IgM-enriched IVIG and monoclonal IVIG, evidence was found to be insufficient to make any conclusions.(2)(64) (EG 1)

For sepsis or septic shock in neonates, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) Systematic reviews and meta-analyses found sufficient evidence that treatment using monoclonal, polyclonal, or IgM-enriched IVIG does not reduce mortality in neonates with proven or suspected sepsis.(64)(65)(66) (EG 1) A multicenter randomized placebo-controlled trial of 3490 infants receiving antibiotics for known or suspected sepsis reported that the addition of IVIG had no effect on adverse events, disability, death, or subsequent septic episodes.(67) (EG 1) For preventing infection in preterm and/or low-birth-weight infants, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 19 studies of approximately 5000 infants concluded that IVIG was effective, with a significant absolute risk reduction of 3% in new cases of sepsis and 4% in one or more episodes of serious infection; however, no significant reductions were observed in other clinical outcomes, including mortality.(68) (EG 1)

For systemic sclerosis with diffuse cutaneous manifestations, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A multicenter randomized controlled study of 63 patients found no statistically significant differences between IVIG and placebo in terms of disease resolution after a single course of IVIG but found some evidence that a second course, given to nonresponders, may be effective. The authors cautioned that such findings are preliminary, and larger randomized studies are necessary.(69) (EG 1) A review article found only a small number of case series or clinical trials that suggested possible improvement in skin, joint, and interstitial lung symptoms for patients with systemic sclerosis and stressed that additional randomized trials are necessary to confirm both effectiveness and dosage.(70) (EG 2) An expert consensus guideline includes IVIG as a potential treatment option for skin involvement in juvenile systemic sclerosis when symptoms are refractory to first-line and second-line therapy.(71) (EG 2)

For transplant of solid organs other than the kidney, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) An expert guideline found insufficient evidence to support the use of IVIG either to prolong graft survival or to treat rejection for heart, lung, or liver transplant.(72) (EG 2) A review article found that evidence is beginning to build support for IVIG in this setting; however, conclusive information is not yet available.(73) (EG 2) A review of therapies for antibody-mediated rejection in cardiac transplant states that IVIG, often in combination with other agents, has never been systematically studied for the purpose of prophylactically reducing the incidence of rejection; however, small case series suggest possible efficacy.(74) (EG 2)

For urticaria that is refractory to standard therapy, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A review article indicates that multiple case reports suggest that IVIG may be useful in patients with chronic disease who are dependent on corticosteroids or cyclosporine, or who may need to discontinue these medications due to side effects; the authors indicate that larger confirmatory studies are necessary.(75) (EG 2) An expert consensus guideline found only case reports and small case series that suggested efficacy of IVIG for autoimmune urticaria; larger controlled studies are needed.(76) (EG 2)

For vasculitis due to antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A systematic review and meta-analysis of 9 studies with 184 patients (including one randomized trial) evaluating IVIG for antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody-associated vasculitis found, in pooled analysis, that IVIG was associated with improvements from baseline in vasculitis activity scores, C-reactive protein, and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody levels. However, the authors noted that the available evidence is limited by a lack of comparative studies and that randomized controlled trials are needed.(77) (EG 1) A meta-analysis evaluating treatment for granulomatosis with polyangiitis found insufficient evidence that IVIG provides a therapeutic advantage to a standard combination of steroids and immunosuppressants, suggesting that its use for this condition be limited to randomized controlled trials.(78) (EG 1) A randomized placebo-controlled trial of 37 patients with antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody-associated microscopic polyangiitis with corticosteroid-refractory neuropathy compared treatment with either IVIG (2 doses given 4 weeks apart) or placebo and found, at 8-week follow-up, no significant between-group differences in change from baseline in manual muscle test score.(79) (EG 1) A specialty society practice guideline states that IVIG should not be used routinely to treat granulomatosis with polyangiitis or microscopic polyangiitis but notes that there may be instances when short-term IVIG may be considered (ie, refractory disease or contraindication to remission induction therapy).(80) (EG 2) An international consensus guideline on the management of antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody-associated vasculitis does not include IVIG as a recommended therapy for induction of remission or maintenance therapy but notes that it may be considered in the setting of diffuse alveolar bleeding with hypoxemia.(81) (EG 2)

For viral encephalitis in children, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A systematic review and meta-analysis of 3 randomized controlled trials (138 patients) evaluating IVIG for treatment of viral encephalitis in pediatric patients found, for children with Japanese encephalitis, no difference in disability or serious adverse events between IVIG and placebo. While there was evidence of clinical benefit of adjunctive IVIG treatment for children with any encephalitis (eg, shorter time to regain consciousness and resolution of symptoms, shorter length of hospital stay), the evidence was deemed low-quality with high risk of bias; the authors could not make firm conclusions regarding the efficacy of this treatment, and further randomized trials were recommended.(82) (EG 1)
