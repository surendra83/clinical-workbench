Clinical Indications for Procedure

Return to top of Hip Arthroplasty - ISC [Expand All / Collapse All]
  • Procedure is indicated for 1 or more of the following(1)(2):
    • Degenerative joint disease, as indicated by
      Degenerative joint disease, as indicated by ALL of the following(1)(2)(5)(6):
      • Moderate to severe radiographic findings (eg, hip joint destruction, severe narrowing, bone deformities, avascular necrosis)[A]
      • Medical management has been tried and failed (eg, weight loss, NSAIDs, physical therapy, activity modification).
      • Arthroplasty indicated due to 1 or more of the following:
        • Moderate to severe pain
        • Functional disability
    • Primary or secondary tumors involving proximal femur(1)(7)
    • Osteonecrosis of femoral head(1)(3)
    • Developmental dysplasia of hip(8)
    • Chronic dislocation of hip(9)(10)
    • Protrusio acetabuli deformity (medial femoral head migration limiting hip motion)(1)
    • Fracture necessitating hip replacement[B](1)(4)(11)(12)(13)
    • Failed previous hip fracture fixation(1)(14)
    • Failed previous femoral or acetabular osteotomy(1)
    • Revision of hip arthrodesis(2)(15)
    • Revision of previous arthroplasty or resurfacing, as indicated by
      Revision of previous arthroplasty or resurfacing, as indicated by 1 or more of the following(1)(2)(16)(17):
      • Instability of one or both components
      • Fracture or mechanical failure of implant
      • Recurrent or irreducible dislocation
      • Infection[C](18)(19)
      • Treatment of periprosthetic fracture(20)
      • Tissue or systemic reaction to metal implant(21)
      • Leg-length inequality(1)

Alternatives to Procedure

Return to top of Hip Arthroplasty - ISC
  • Alternatives include(1)(2)(9)(22):
    • Optimal nonoperative management, which may include(6)(8)(22)(26):
      • Anti-inflammatory medication
      • Analgesics
      • Tumor necrosis factor inhibitors
      • Flexibility and muscle strengthening exercises
      • Physical therapy
      • Reasonable restriction of activities
      • Ambulatory aids (eg, cane, walker)
      • Weight reduction if appropriate
      • Chronic antibiotic suppression for infection
    • Irrigation and debridement of infected prosthetic joint
    • Pelvic osteotomy(9)
    • Femoral or periacetabular osteotomy(1)(8)(9)(24)
    • Open reduction and internal fixation (eg, for selected femoral neck, trochanteric, or acetabular fractures)(1)
    • Hip arthrodesis(25)
    • Core decompression of femoral head. See Hip: Core Decompression of Femoral Head guideline.
    • Hip resurfacing. See Hip Resurfacing guideline.
    • Open repair of hip fracture. See Hip Fracture, Open Repair guideline.
    • Hemiarthroplasty. See Hip: Displaced Fracture of Femoral Neck, Hemiarthroplasty guideline.

Goal Length of Stay: Ambulatory

Note: The definition of an ambulatory procedure depends on payer-provider contractual agreement or regulatory language (eg, CMS' Two-Midnight Rule). An ambulatory procedure may include one postoperative overnight stay in a facility; therefore, MCG's ambulatory Goal Length of Stay (GLOS) attainment calculation reports the sum of same-day and next-day postoperative discharges. Depending on various patient and procedural factors, some patients undergoing a procedure with an ambulatory GLOS require inpatient care (eg, medical necessity for hospital-based care across 2 or more postoperative midnights). Some of these factors are described in the Extended Stay section of this guideline.

Extended Stay

Return to top of Hip Arthroplasty - ISC

Minimal (a few hours to 1 day), Brief (1 to 3 days), Moderate (4 to 7 days), and Prolonged (more than 7 days).

  • Inpatient stay (eg, need for hospital-based care beyond postoperative day 1) may be needed for(1)(33):
    • Failure to meet discharge criteria (recovery milestones in Optimal Recovery Course)
      • Expect brief stay extension.
    • Preoperative infection or periprosthetic fracture of previously placed hip joint implant
      • Expect brief stay extension.
    • Arthroplasty necessary due to femur fracture(34)(35)
      • Expect brief stay extension.
    • Venous thromboembolism or pulmonary embolism(36)(37)
      • Anticipate anticoagulation.
      • Expect brief stay extension.
      • See Venous Thrombosis and Pulmonary Embolism: Common Complications and Conditions guideline as appropriate.
    • Postoperative dislocation
      • Expect minimal to brief stay extension.
    • Peroneal, sciatic, obturator, or femoral nerve injury
      • Physical therapy and rehabilitation may be necessary.
      • Expect brief stay extension.
    • Hemorrhage or hematoma formation
      • Anticipate antibiotics to prevent secondary infection, hematoma evacuation, and vessel repair.
      • Expect brief to moderate stay extension.
    • Comorbidities (eg, heart failure, COPD, renal failure, anemia, neurologic disease, collagen vascular disease, cirrhosis) necessitating prolonged postoperative treatment or monitoring(38)
      • Comorbidities may require extended inpatient care.
      • Expect brief stay extension.
    • American Society of Anesthesiologist (ASA) class 4 or 5(39)
      • Patients with higher ASA scores have a higher incidence of postoperative complications.
      • Expect brief stay extension.
    • Frail patient (eg, Hospital Frailty Risk Score of 5 or greater)(40)
      • Frail patients are at higher risk for medical and surgical complications and have greater need for a postoperative inpatient rehabilitation facility.
      • Expect brief stay extension.
    • Complications of procedure(37)(41)(42)
      • Complications (eg, postoperative MI, pneumonia, Acute kidney injury (stage 2), Acute renal failure (stage 3 acute kidney injury), infected prosthesis, vascular injury) may require ongoing inpatient care and possible surgical intervention.
      • Expect brief to moderate stay extension depending on condition.
    • Postoperative delirium(43)
      • Anticipate delayed activity progression as well as evaluation and possible treatment of underlying cause.
      • Expect brief stay extension.
      • See Mental Status Change: Common Complications and Conditions guideline as appropriate.
    • Avascular necrosis or osteonecrosis indication for procedure (eg, sickle cell disease)(3)(44)
      • Medical manifestations of sickling (eg, painful crisis, acute chest syndrome) and higher rates of surgical complications (eg, infection, wound complications) may require further inpatient stay.
      • Expect brief to moderate stay extension.
    • Bilateral arthroplasty
      • Expect brief stay extension.

See Common Complications and Conditions for further information.

Evidence Summary

Return to top of Hip Arthroplasty - ISC

Criteria

Return to top of Hip Arthroplasty - ISC

The evidence for the clinical indications found in this guideline includes 19 published peer reviewed articles, 1 specialty society or other evidence-based guideline, and 1 book section.

For degenerative joint disease associated with disabling pain or functional disability that has failed optimal medical management, an orthopedic surgery textbook and a narrative review support total hip arthroplasty to improve symptoms and function.(1)(2) (EG 2)

For primary or secondary tumors involving the proximal femur, protrusio acetabuli deformity, failed previous femoral or acetabular osteotomy procedure, or for an acute fracture that is not amenable to more conservative measures, an orthopedic surgery textbook recommends total hip arthroplasty.(1) (EG 2)

For osteonecrosis of the femoral head, a systematic review supports total hip arthroplasty for patients with sickle cell disease.(3) (EG 2)

For hip fracture, a specialty society guideline recommends arthroplasty over fixation for displaced (unstable) femoral neck fractures.(4) (EG 2) An orthopedic surgery textbook states that patients who have trochanteric fractures with comminution, significant displacement, poor bone quality, or pre-existing damaged hip joints or bone disease (eg, arthritis, Paget disease, avascular necrosis of the femoral head) may have a poor response to internal fixation and therefore should be considered for total hip arthroplasty.(1) (EG 2)

For revision of previous arthroplasty or resurfacing, an orthopedic surgery textbook and a narrative review support total hip arthroplasty.(1)(2) (EG 2) The narrative review states that the most common indications for revision surgery are mechanical failure of the implant, recurrent dislocation, infection, and periprosthetic fracture.(2) (EG 2)

Alternatives

Return to top of Hip Arthroplasty - ISC

A meta-analysis of 16 studies encompassing 3084 patients with displaced femoral neck fracture randomized to either total hip arthroplasty or hemiarthroplasty found similar functional outcomes with similar rates of postoperative revision, dislocation, periprosthetic fracture, and mortality; although there was a significant difference in health-related quality-of-life measures favoring total hip arthroplasty, this was less than the predetermined minimally important difference.(12) (EG 1) A specialty society guideline considers hemiarthroplasty as a reasonable alternative to total hip arthroplasty for selected patients with displaced femoral neck fracture because it may have improved functional benefit; however, they also warn that hemiarthroplasty has a higher risk of complications.(4) (EG 2) Osteotomy may be indicated for femoroacetabular impingement or developmental dysplasia of the hip, especially for dysplasia patients without significant secondary osteoarthritis, and for impingement patients for whom arthroscopy is either not appropriate or not sufficiently effective.(8)(9)(23)(24) (EG 2) Arthrodesis may be indicated for patients with severe unilateral hip arthritis for whom joint preserving and joint replacement procedures are not appropriate.(25) (EG 2)

Hospitalization

Return to top of Hip Arthroplasty - ISC

Specialty society guidelines recommend administration of tranexamic acid for patients undergoing total hip or knee arthroplasty based on meta-analyses of randomized controlled studies which showed that administration of tranexamic acid reduced blood loss and need for transfusion.(29)(32) (EG 1)

Length of Stay

Return to top of Hip Arthroplasty - ISC

Examination of a Medicare fee-for-service database found that, over the time period of January 2021 to March 2021, 68% of non-fracture total hip arthroplasties were performed on an outpatient basis.(30) (EG 2) Analysis of procedure data for a commercially insured population shows 93% of patients undergoing hip arthroplasty were discharged the day of or the day after surgery.(31) (EG 3) Analysis of procedure data for a Medicare-insured population shows 77% of patients undergoing hip arthroplasty were discharged the day of or the day after surgery.(31) (EG 3)

Rationale

Return to top of Hip Arthroplasty - ISC

Use of this MCG care guideline helps the clinician identify, for a given procedure, which patient-specific factors and clinical conditions are appropriate for that procedure. The evidence-based clinical criteria assist the clinician in the decision to appropriately perform a procedure, evaluating whether the potential benefits of a procedure outweigh the potential risks. For Medicare enrollees, surgical MCG care guidelines also identify which procedures CMS has designated as inpatient only.

Use of these evidence-based clinical criteria to support decision making around the need for a given procedure is of benefit to the patient, as all procedures come with inherent risk that must be balanced by anticipated clinical benefit. Utilizing evidence-based clinical criteria enables a more accurate and patient-specific decision-making process. In addition, the use of evidence-based guidelines can help reduce unwarranted variation in care, such as divergent clinical thresholds to perform a procedure for clinically similar patients that vary across geographic regions, between facilities, and among individual clinicians.
