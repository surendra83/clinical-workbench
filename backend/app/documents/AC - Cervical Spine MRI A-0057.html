        • Weight loss, unexplained(42)(43)

Evidence Summary

Background

Return to top of Cervical Spine MRI - AC

Some gadolinium-based contrast agents used for diagnostic and treatment guidance with MRI or MRA have been associated with nephrogenic systemic fibrosis in patients with chronic kidney disease or acute kidney injury.(6)(7)(8)(9)(10) (EG 2) Gadolinium deposition in the brain has been reported by multiple research groups; however, the clinical significance of this finding is unclear.(6)(7)(11)(12) (EG 2) Specialty society guidelines recommend weighing the risks vs benefits when using gadolinium-based contrast agents for MRI.(13) (EG 2) MRI-based techniques may be contraindicated in patients with ferromagnetic intracranial aneurysm clips and certain types of ferromagnetic foreign bodies and cochlear implants.(14) (EG 2) Although these implants have traditionally included cardiac pacemakers and implantable cardioverter-defibrillators, multiple trials have validated the safe and effective use of pacemaker systems designed for MRI use, and a guideline supported by multiple specialty societies states that many patients with implantable cardiac devices, including those not originally intended for MRI scanning, can safely undergo MRI scanning under defined clinical indications.(7)(15)(16)(17)(18)(19) (EG 1)

Criteria

Return to top of Cervical Spine MRI - AC

The evidence for the clinical indications found in this guideline includes 202 published peer reviewed articles, 10 specialty society or other evidence-based guidelines, and 13 book sections.

For cancer or neoplasm evaluation or staging, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) Whole-spine MRI is the preferred imaging modality for suspected metastatic disease to the spine in patients with known primary malignancy; metastatic epidural spinal cord compression is seen in 5% to 10% of patients with cancer.(26)(27) (EG 2) Spinal cord tumor in children may present with persistent localized back pain that is worse at night.(28)(29)(30) (EG 2) Additional findings in children with spinal cord tumor may include burning pain with paresthesia, gait disturbance, paraspinal muscle spasm, progressive motor weakness, progressive scoliosis, and sensory deficits; cervical spine lesions may cause obstruction of the foramen magnum leading to symptoms of increased intracranial pressure.(31) (EG 2)

For cerebrospinal fluid leak, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) A specialty society guideline notes that MRI of the spine may be useful to discover the location of a cerebrospinal fluid leak in patients with intracranial hypotension.(20) (EG 2)

For Chiari malformation, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Cervical spine MRI is indicated to diagnose miscellaneous congenital spinal deformities, including Chiari malformation and spinal dysraphisms.(61)(63) (EG 2)

For congenital spinal conditions, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) A specialty society guideline indicates that MRI is an appropriate imaging modality for assessment of congenital spinal conditions.(20) (EG 2)

For infection of the spine, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) MRI is a preferred option for diagnosis of suspected spinal infection and inflammation, including spondylitis, epidural abscess, osteomyelitis, and spinal tuberculosis.(83)(84)(85)(86)(87) (EG 2) Because spinal tuberculosis can present in noncontiguous vertebral bodies, patients with known spinal tuberculosis should undergo MRI imaging of the entire spine.(88) (EG 2)

For inflammatory or demyelinating processes, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Diagnostic criteria have been developed for inflammatory or demyelinating processes, such as clinically isolated syndrome or multiple sclerosis (most commonly manifested as unilateral optic neuritis), brainstem syndrome, or partial myelitis, based on the demonstration of central nervous system lesions disseminated in space and disseminated in time on MRI imaging of the brain and, if appropriate, on spinal cord imaging.(100)(101)(102)(103)(104)(105) (EG 2) Spinal cord involvement is seen on MRI imaging in 80% to 90% of patients with multiple sclerosis; asymptomatic spinal cord lesions may be present in up to 40% of patients with clinically isolated syndrome, with lesions occurring more commonly in the cervical spine than in the thoracic spine.(106)(107)(108)(109) (EG 2) An expert consensus panel recommends spinal cord imaging for patients with suspected demyelinating disease who present with signs or symptoms of spinal cord involvement or if the number of lesions on brain MRI is insufficient to meet established criteria for multiple sclerosis.(100) (EG 2)

For Klippel-Feil syndrome, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Klippel-Feil syndrome can predispose to cervical spondylotic myelopathy later in life.(119)(123)(124) (EG 2) Performing cervical spine MRI and CT scan in Klippel-Feil syndrome and other congenital cervical anomalies may help to identify the presence and degree of craniovertebral dislocation and atlantoaxial rotatory subluxation, which may present as acute spinal cord compression in children.(120)(121)(125) (EG 2)

For pain localized to the neck or radicular in nature, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) Review articles consider MRI an appropriate initial imaging modality for pain that significantly interferes with daily function and for which invasive treatment is being considered.(126)(127)(128) (EG 2) A specialty society guideline states that MRI may be appropriate for the evaluation of acute or increasing cervical pain with radiculopathy and for chronic cervical pain.(41) (EG 2) For children and adolescents with back or neck pain, a specialty society guideline notes that, while imaging is not generally indicated for pain that is self-limited or not associated with signs or symptoms of an underlying disorder, MRI is appropriate for evaluating pain associated with potential red flags (eg, associated gait abnormalities, unintentional weight loss, persistence over more than 4 weeks).(30) (EG 2)

For postoperative spinal complications, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Review articles indicate that MRI is effective at demonstrating common postoperative complications after spinal surgery.(140)(141)(142) (EG 2)

For rheumatoid arthritis, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) The prevalence of cervical spine involvement in rheumatoid arthritis ranges from 16% to 70%, presenting as atlantoaxial subluxation, subaxial subluxation, or cranial settling.(146) (EG 1) MRI is the most useful test for patients with signs and symptoms suggestive of cervical spondylotic myelopathy; however, if MRI is contraindicated, CT myelography is recommended.(64)(143) (EG 2)

For scoliosis, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Review articles conclude that MRI is not routinely indicated for pain in the absence of abnormal neurologic examination findings or an atypical curve, such as a left-sided curve (levoscoliosis), a short segment, or kyphosis near the curve apex.(149)(150)(151)(152) (EG 2) However, it is useful preoperatively in evaluating the neural axis.(153) (EG 2)

For spinal cord compression, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) A systematic review concluded that MRI is appropriate for diagnostic confirmation of cervical spondylotic myelopathy.(158) (EG 2) MRI is the preferred imaging modality for detection and confirmation of malignant spinal cord compression.(46) (EG 2) Literature reviews of acute transverse myelitis recommend urgent neuroimaging with MRI to rule out spinal cord compression; however, if MRI is not available or is contraindicated, CT myelography is indicated.(99)(116) (EG 2)

For spinal cord infarction or ischemia, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) MRI of the spine is the preferred diagnostic procedure to detect spinal cord ischemia. The clinical manifestations may evolve over minutes to hours, vary depending on the vascular territory involved, may be bilateral or unilateral, and may present transiently (also called spinal transient ischemic attacks).(159)(160)(161) (EG 2) Etiologies may vary (eg, aortic dissection, atherosclerosis, coagulopathy, embolization, mechanical compression, systemic hypoperfusion, vasculitis).(159)(161)(162)(163) (EG 2) For patients experiencing spinal infarct or ischemia, timely identification facilitates reperfusion intervention, which may improve outcomes.(160) (EG 2)

For spinal cord stimulator placement planning, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Preoperative MRI of the spine is recommended within 12 months prior to the spinal cord stimulator placement to evaluate potential pathology that could alter the diagnosis or negatively impact the outcome and to assess the need for alternative approaches to placement of the stimulator electrodes.(166)(167)(168) (EG 2)

For spinal dysraphism, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Spinal dysraphism includes a variety of malformations with incomplete fusion of midline mesenchymal, bony, and neurologic structures; a broad bony defect of the vertebral arch is seen at the level of the malformation.(63)(72)(170) (EG 2) Spinal dysraphism may be associated with neurogenic scoliosis. Intraspinal pathology has been reported in 12% to 50% of patients with congenital scoliosis; the MRI findings may include anomalies such as filum lipoma, syrinx, or Chiari malformation.(150)(174) (EG 2)

For spinal stenosis of the cervical spine, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) A literature review on the imaging of spinal stenosis noted that CT scan, CT myelography, and MRI can all visualize the ventral and dorsal contributions to spinal canal compromise.(180) (EG 2)

For spondylolysis, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Prior to the development of detectable stress fracture on plain radiograph or CT scan, bone marrow edema of the pars interarticularis may be identified using thin-slice MRI in patients with spondylolysis.(30)(183)(184) (EG 2) MRI may also detect associated ligament disruption in traumatic spondylolysis and is used to evaluate neurologic findings, such as radiculopathy, in patients with spondylolysis.(30)(185)(186) (EG 2)

For stereotactic spine radiotherapy, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) CT scan and MRI are increasingly used for treatment planning prior to stereotactic spine radiotherapy for spinal metastases.(187)(188)(190)(192)(193) (EG 2)

For syringomyelia in the cervical spine, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) A specialty society guideline indicates that MRI is an appropriate imaging modality for assessment.(20) (EG 2) A literature review recommends that when Chiari malformation is suspected on cranial CT scan, MRI of the entire spine is indicated.(195) (EG 2)

For torticollis in children, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Review articles indicate that MRI of the brain and cervical spine is an appropriate imaging study in children presenting with torticollis in the absence of trauma, after an initial negative CT scan of the neck and cervical spine. In newborns and infants with suspected congenital torticollis, ultrasound is the preferred initial imaging study.(197) (EG 2) MRI is recommended for cases in which atypical features on ultrasound warrant further evaluation.(122)(197) (EG 2)

For trauma or fracture of the cervical spine, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) MRI is helpful in the assessment of acute and subacute spinal cord trauma and can demonstrate ligamentous and cord injury, displaced disk fragments, and intraspinal hematomas not visible on CT scan.(202)(203)(204)(205)(206) (EG 1) For blunt cervical trauma, a meta-analysis concluded that cervical spine MRI has a negative predictive value of 100% for spinal cord injury and is especially useful to evaluate trauma patients who are obtunded or have an altered mental status when CT scan is negative or nondiagnostic.(204) (EG 1) Systematic reviews of studies of patients with spinal cord injuries who did not have abnormalities on plain x-rays or CT scans have demonstrated abnormal spine MRI findings in 57% of children and 93% of adults.(207)(208) (EG 1) A meta-analysis of 1714 clinically unevaluable blunt trauma patients with initial negative CT scans of the cervical spine noted abnormal MRI scans in 271 patients (15.7%); however, only 5 patients (0.3%) required surgical intervention.(209) (EG 1) A prospective study of 767 blunt trauma patients who either were clinically unevaluable or had persistent midline cervicalgia after initial negative CT scans of the cervical spine found that 181 (23.6%) had an abnormal cervical spine MRI. Of these 181 patients, 11 underwent cervical spine surgery after review of the MRI results; however, 6 of the 11 had neurologic signs or symptoms, and the authors concluded that the clinical significance of the additional injuries noted on MRI was unclear.(210) (EG 2) Retrospective cohort studies of patients with spinal cord injury without radiographic abnormalities concluded that early cervical spine MRI may help predict neurologic outcomes.(211)(212) (EG 2) A retrospective cohort study of 99 patients with cervical fractures determined that MRI detected abnormalities not seen on CT scan in 48% of cases, which led to a change in management in 39% of cases. Factors associated with abnormalities on MRI included age of 61 years or older, neurologic deficit, multiple trauma status, decreased alertness, and cervical spondylosis.(213) (EG 2) A prospective cohort study of 53 children younger than 36 months with documented inflicted head trauma found that 15% had associated cervical spine injuries noted on cervical spine MRI.(214) (EG 2) In a retrospective cohort study of 206 pediatric patients evaluated for spinal injuries in a trauma center, MRI was obtained more frequently in patients between 0 and 3 years of age (67%) as compared with patients between 4 and 9 years of age (20%). The authors hypothesized that this disparity may be due to difficulty in obtaining reliable neurologic examinations in infants and toddlers. MRI is also a better tool for imaging cartilage, of which a significant portion of the spinal column in this age group is composed.(215) (EG 2) A retrospective study of 84 children with cervical trauma found that, when CT scan was used as the standard, MRI identified fractures with sensitivity of 100% and specificity of 97%. The authors recommended that larger, prospective studies be performed to investigate the potential role of MRI in the diagnosis of fractures.(216) (EG 2) A case control study compared spine MRI results with intraoperative findings in 35 patients and found that MRI had sensitivity of 80% to 100% and specificity of 64% to 100% for diagnosis of ligamentous, intervertebral disk, and paraspinous muscle injuries.(217) (EG 2)

For tuberculosis of the spine, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) MRI is considered the most accurate imaging modality for diagnosis and classification of tuberculosis, including early spinal cord changes.(88)(243) (EG 2)
