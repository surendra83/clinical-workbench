Background

Return to top of Brain MRI - AC

Some gadolinium-based contrast agents used for diagnostic and treatment guidance with MRI or MRA have been associated with nephrogenic systemic fibrosis in patients with chronic kidney disease or acute kidney injury.(4)(5)(6)(7)(8) (EG 2) Gadolinium deposition in the brain has been reported by multiple research groups; however, the clinical significance of this finding is unclear.(7)(8)(9)(10)(11) (EG 2) Specialty society guidelines recommend weighing the risks vs benefits when using gadolinium-based contrast agents for MRI.(12) (EG 2) MRI-based techniques may be contraindicated in patients with ferromagnetic intracranial aneurysm clips and certain types of ferromagnetic foreign bodies and cochlear implants.(13) (EG 2) Although these implants have traditionally included cardiac pacemakers and implantable cardioverter-defibrillators, multiple trials have validated the safe and effective use of pacemaker systems designed for MRI use, and a guideline supported by multiple specialty societies states that many patients with implantable cardiac devices, including those not originally intended for MRI scanning, can safely undergo MRI scanning under defined clinical indications.(14)(15)(16)(17) (EG 1)

The evidence for the clinical indications found in this guideline includes 243 published peer reviewed articles, 30 specialty society or other evidence-based guidelines, 2 Cochrane systematic reviews, and 7 book sections.

For acoustic neuromas (vestibular schwannomas), evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) MRI can characterize acoustic neuromas (vestibular schwannomas) with a high level of resolution, allowing for a more conservative approach to treatment using serial follow-up MRI to track tumor growth.(22) (EG 2) A retrospective chart review of 276 patients showed that 84% of the tumors most dangerous to patients (ie, those that grow most rapidly) were detected by the initial follow-up MRI at 6 months.(23) (EG 2)

For anatomy or structural defect evaluation, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) Review article authors consider MRI to be the imaging modality of choice for malformations of the midbrain and hindbrain due to its ability to determine the location, degree, and extent of the malformation and identify associated supratentorial congenital anomalies.(25) (EG 2)

For anosmia, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Although CT scan may be useful for the evaluation of suspected obstructive causes of anosmia and visualization of nasal cavities, sinuses, and bony abnormalities, MRI can visualize the entire olfactory system as well as the temporal and frontal lobes; both tumors and vascular lesions of the temporal lobe can cause anosmia.(47) (EG 2) Anosmia is a common symptom of Parkinson disease and may precede motor symptoms by several years as part of a prodromal phase.(50)(51) (EG 2)

For cancer or neoplasm evaluation or staging, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) Review articles and clinical guidelines state that MRI of the brain and spine is the gold standard for imaging of the central nervous system.(60)(61)(62)(63)(64) (EG 2) For pituitary or sellar tumor, an expert consensus guideline and a review article recommend MRI as first-line imaging for neuroendocrine disease of the pituitary or hypothalamus.(65)(66) (EG 2) For pituitary incidentaloma, a specialty society guideline recommends that patients with pituitary tumors incidentally noted on unrelated imaging (also known as pituitary incidentalomas) who have not undergone surgical treatment should be followed by MRI imaging at intervals determined by the size of the incidentaloma.(67) (EG 2)

For cerebral edema, including cytotoxic, vasogenic, interstitial, or combined etiologies, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) Review articles indicate that MRI and CT scan offer complementary methods for evaluating underlying soft tissue lesion pathology.(137)(138) (EG 2)

For cerebrospinal fluid leak, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Brain MRI is recommended as part of the workup for suspected cerebrospinal fluid leak in patients with intracranial hypotension.(139)(140) (EG 2) Imaging findings suggestive of cerebrospinal fluid leak include pachymeningeal enhancement, engorged venous sinus, effacement of suprasellar and prepontine cisterns, subdural fluid collections, and decreased mamillopontine distance.(141) (EG 2)

For delirium, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) MRI is the imaging modality of choice for evaluating patients with altered mental status or suspected encephalopathy, although CT scan is often the initial investigation due to its availability and rapid performance.(143)(144) (EG 2) Specific toxic exposures, such as cyanide, ethylene glycol, methanol, and carbon monoxide, can cause characteristic MRI changes; imaging may be useful in managing patients with a complicated presentation.(145) (EG 2)

For dementia, known or suspected, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) For Alzheimer disease, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 33 studies (including 3935 participants with mild cognitive impairment) evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of structural MRI for early Alzheimer dementia and found that MRI cortical volume measurements had pooled sensitivity and specificity of 73% and 71%, respectively, for hippocampus and pooled sensitivity and specificity of 64% and 65%, respectively, for medial temporal lobe. The authors concluded that brain MRI was insufficient as a stand-alone diagnostic test for Alzheimer disease; however, initial brain imaging was recommended to exclude other structural etiologies of dementia. Findings were limited by small study populations, interstudy heterogeneity, risk for bias, and low certainty of evidence; further larger, well-designed studies were recommended.(151) (EG 1) An expert consensus panel recommends performance of brain imaging once in every patient with suspected Alzheimer disease to exclude other structural etiologies of dementia and confirm specific imaging findings consistent with Alzheimer disease.(152) (EG 2) An open-label extension study of 2 clinical trials evaluating bapineuzumab for patients with mild to moderate Alzheimer disease further investigated the risk for the adverse effect of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), manifested as hemosiderin deposition (ARIA-H) or edema (ARIA-E), with potential for adjusted management. The investigators reviewed MRI results from 262 study participants and found that ARIA was associated with the presence of the APOE e4 alleles (hazard ratio 11.9 and 3.5 for 2 and 1 alleles, respectively), treatment with bapineuzumab therapy (hazard ratio 3.5), and the use of antithrombotic agents (hazard ratio 2.2). Findings were limited by potential for bias with retrospective analysis and variations in dosing and MRI protocols; further well-designed trials were recommended.(153) (EG 2) A health technology assessment concluded that brain imaging is most helpful in cases where the cause of dementia remains unclear despite prolonged follow-up (eg, 2 years or more); both CT scan and MRI can assist in detecting a vascular component of dementia.(154) (EG 2) An expert consensus panel recommends that a suspected diagnosis of vascular cognitive dementia be confirmed by evidence of significant cerebrovascular disease on either brain CT scan or MRI.(155) (EG 2) A consensus guideline states that brain MRI is preferable to brain CT to evaluate an individual with dementia if the presence of unsuspected cerebrovascular disease would change the clinical management; other indications for neuroimaging include duration of dementia less than 2 years, unexpected or unexplained cognitive or functional decline in a patient already diagnosed with dementia, recent significant head trauma, risk for intracranial bleeding, history of cancer with risk for brain metastases, or associated significant neurologic signs or symptoms (eg, gait abnormality, seizure, upper motor neuron signs).(156) (EG 2) A systematic review and meta-analysis indicates that CT scan is useful for exclusion of a potentially surgically treatable cause of dementia, such as tumor or subdural hematoma, as well as for the evaluation of the presence and extent of cerebrovascular disease.(157) (EG 1) However, MRI is more beneficial for identification of specific markers of dementing diseases, such as patterns of cerebral atrophy.(158)(159)(160)(161)(162) (EG 2) MRI is considered the preferred imaging modality for assistance with early diagnosis of dementia.(146)(163)(164) (EG 2) MRI may be useful to confirm dementia due to specific metabolic disorders, such as Wilson disease.(145)(165)(166) (EG 2) MRI is indicated before initiation of antiamyloid monoclonal antibody therapy in patients with early Alzheimer disease to evaluate for findings associated higher risk of ARIA (eg, microhemorrhages), for surveillance for ARIA during antiamyloid monoclonal antibody therapy, and to evaluate for stabilization or worsening of known ARIA.(148)(167) (EG 2)

For demyelinating disease (eg, multiple sclerosis), evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) In patients presenting with a clinically isolated syndrome, such as unilateral optic neuritis, brainstem syndrome, or partial myelitis, diagnostic criteria for multiple sclerosis have been developed based on the demonstration of central nervous system lesions disseminated in space and disseminated in time on MRI imaging of the brain and, if appropriate, on spinal cord imaging.(177)(178) (EG 2) Requirements for radiographic dissemination in time criteria for multiple sclerosis include either the simultaneous presence of both gadolinium-enhancing (new) and nonenhancing (older) lesions on the same MRI, or new T2-weighted or gadolinium-enhancing lesions on a repeat MRI performed at any time after the initial onset of symptoms.(176)(177)(178) (EG 2) However, in some cases there is still significant inter-rater variability in assessing MRI results, and work continues to improve standardization of assessment of imaging findings, especially with regard to prognostic implications.(179)(180)(181)(182) (EG 2) A prospective cohort study of 1139 MRI scans in children and adolescents with acute central nervous system demyelination found that specific MRI parameters (eg, one or more T1-weighted hypointense lesions or one or more periventricular lesions) could be used to predict the diagnosis of multiple sclerosis.(183) (EG 2) Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder is often characterized by periependymal lesions located around the ventricular system or lesions involving the corticospinal tracts on brain MRI.(184) (EG 2)

For developmental delay, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) A retrospective review of hospital records of 580 developmentally delayed children found that 58.6% of cases had an abnormal brain MRI; the authors concluded that the use of brain MRI may be effective for the diagnosis and management of developmental delay.(191) (EG 2) Review articles state that brain MRI may be useful in the evaluation of inborn errors of metabolism, which may present with neurologic symptoms that include developmental delay.(165)(194) (EG 2)

For dizziness or vertigo, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) MRI is a standard and widely accepted imaging modality for the evaluation of dizziness or vertigo in the setting of other potentially ominous signs or symptoms.(195)(196)(197)(198) (EG 2)

For epilepsy or seizure disorder, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) Consensus guidelines recommend neuroimaging with either brain MRI or CT scan, preferably the former when it is available.(200)(201)(205) (EG 2) For children with epilepsy, an expert consensus guideline recommends neuroimaging with brain MRI for the following: status epilepticus; failure to control seizures, worsening seizures, or changes in seizure manifestations; seizures in children younger than 2 years, excluding those with febrile seizures; abnormal neurologic examination; a history of significant developmental delay, arrest, or regression; or a new-onset focal seizure.(200) (EG 2) A systematic review of studies using neuroimaging to identify patients with drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy who might benefit from focal cortical resection surgery found that the outcome was more favorable in patients with preoperative brain MRI demonstrating mesial temporal sclerosis; the authors concluded that brain MRI plays a pivotal role in evaluating surgical candidates with drug-resistant focal epilepsy of temporal lobe origin.(206) (EG 1) A systematic review of studies reporting on outcomes of epilepsy surgery found that an abnormal brain MRI, especially if it correlated with EEG findings, was more predictive of a good surgical result than a normal MRI.(207) (EG 1) A retrospective study of 177 children age 3 to 18 years who presented with new-onset status epilepticus reported abnormal findings on brain CT scan or MRI in 36%; 8.5% had urgent or emergent pathology.(208) (EG 2)

For headache with a possible underlying structural cause, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Brain neuroimaging is indicated for those headaches associated with concerning signs or symptoms, including an increase in frequency or severity, description as the worst headache of the patient's life (eg, "thunderclap" headache), fever and meningeal signs, motor or sensory aura, and abnormal findings on neurologic examination.(215) (EG 2)

For hearing loss, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) A specialty society guideline and review articles state that MRI is appropriate for the detection of acoustic neuroma (vestibular schwannoma) in appropriately selected patients, such as those with unilateral sensorineural hearing loss.(195)(196)(197) (EG 1)

For infection, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Brain neuroimaging is especially helpful in assessing potential brain abscesses, immunosuppression-related conditions, and viral or bacterial encephalitides.(223)(225)(228)(229)(230) (EG 2) When meningitis is suspected and the patient is at risk for an intracranial mass or midline shift, a head CT should be obtained before lumbar puncture is performed due to the risk of brain herniation.(225)(228) (EG 2) In uncomplicated acute meningitis, contrast-enhanced CT scan of the head may show leptomeningeal enhancement, though MRI of the brain tends to be more sensitive, particularly near the calvarium.(231) (EG 2)

For intracranial vasculitis, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) MRI may demonstrate previous infarcts in different vascular distributions, suggestive of vasculitis. MRI is the most sensitive imaging modality for detecting the changes in cerebral vasculitis involving large, medium, and small vessels, with sensitivity estimated to be close to 100%; however, its specificity is much lower.(247)(248)(252) (EG 2)

For neonatal hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) A systematic review and meta-analysis of the prognostic value of tests for term neonates with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy found that brain MRI performed during the first week of life had a pooled sensitivity and specificity of 84% and 90%, respectively; diffusion-weighted MRI in the first week of life had a pooled specificity of 89% but a pooled sensitivity of only 58%.(254) (EG 1) MRI is the preferred imaging study for neonates with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, and imaging abnormalities have been found to correlate with neurologic outcomes.(253) (EG 2)

For neurologic disease signs or symptoms, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Brain neuroimaging is indicated to help identify possible underlying causes when concerning neurologic signs or symptoms are present.(49)(250) (EG 2)

For Parkinson disease and other neurodegenerative conditions, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) A meta-analysis of 10 studies with 595 patients found that the absence of dorsolateral nigral hyperintensity on iron-sensitive MRI sequences had sensitivity of 97.7% and specificity of 94.6% for differentiating Parkinson disease from healthy control patients.(264) (EG 1) MRI is helpful in differentiating Parkinson disease from atypical Parkinson disease and can provide additional evidence for the presence of other conditions, such as progressive supranuclear palsy.(261)(265)(266) (EG 2) MRI changes such as atrophy of the caudate nuclei may precede characteristic chorea in patients with Huntington disease by several years, facilitating a clinical diagnosis.(165)(267) (EG 2) Appropriate-use criteria state that brain MRI is an appropriate first test for suspected Parkinson or Huntington disease or dementia with Lewy bodies.(150)(268) (EG 2)

For precocious puberty (central), evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Brain MRI is indicated to rule out hypothalamic lesions such as hamartoma.(65)(272)(273) (EG 2)

For preterm infant needing near-term equivalent (ie, 37 to 42 weeks' gestation) evaluation, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) A systematic review of 20 cohort studies with 1287 infants younger than 32 weeks' gestation, or less than 1500 g birth weight, evaluated 3 different findings (white matter abnormalities, brain abnormality, and diffuse excessive high signal intensity) noted on brain MRI performed at 37 to 42 weeks' gestation. Taken together, these findings had a pooled sensitivity of 77% and specificity of 79% for predicting cerebral palsy at age 18 months or older; for predicting motor dysfunction, brain MRI had a pooled sensitivity and specificity of 72% and 62%, respectively.(274) (EG 1) A prospective cohort study of 480 preterm infants (less than 28 weeks' gestational age) found that abnormalities seen on near-term (35 to 42 weeks' gestation) brain MRI or cranial ultrasound were associated with poorer neurodevelopmental outcomes at 18 to 22 months' corrected age; MRI with cerebellar abnormalities had a higher association with adverse outcomes. The authors concluded that neuroimaging at near-term equivalent for extremely preterm infants had value in predicting neurodevelopmental abnormalities at age 18 to 22 months.(275) (EG 2) A review article notes that brain MRI at term-equivalent age improves the prediction of later developmental disabilities; however, further research is needed.(277) (EG 2)

For sickle disease evaluation for stroke prophylaxis, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) A randomized controlled study of 196 children (mean age 10 years) with sickle cell disease, no history of stroke, and one or more silent cerebral infarcts on MRI compared the efficacy of standard care vs regular red blood cell transfusions to maintain a target hemoglobin concentration. At a median follow-up of 3 years, transfusion was associated with a lower incidence of stroke or new or enlarged silent infarct; the authors recommended that the detection of a silent cerebral infarct in a child with sickle cell anemia be followed by a discussion about treatment options.(279) (EG 1)

For stroke (ischemic) or transient ischemic attack, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) An expert consensus guideline recommends urgent brain MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging, preferably within 24 hours of symptom onset of stroke or transient ischemic attack.(285) (EG 2) Expert consensus guidelines and review articles recommend urgent brain imaging such as CT scan, or MRI if available and not contraindicated, for acute ischemic stroke prior to initiating any specific therapy, including thrombolytic medication.(250)(286)(287)(288) (EG 2) However, an evidence-based specialty society guideline recommends against routine brain MRI to exclude cerebral microbleeds before administration of thrombolytic medication; the guideline also notes that multimodal brain MRI should not delay thrombolytic administration.(280) (EG 2) An evidence-based national specialty society guideline recommends brain MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging for the diagnosis of ischemic stroke within 12 hours of symptom onset.(281) (EG 2) A consensus group recommends that neonates with suspected stroke be evaluated with MRI due to the lack of radiation.(289) (EG 2) A systematic review of 47 studies including 9078 patients with clinically diagnosed transient ischemic attack found that brain MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging demonstrated an acute ischemic lesion in only 34.3% of patients, with significant heterogeneity among studies; the authors concluded that MRI with diffusion-weighted MRI was not useful as a test to reclassify transient ischemic attack as stroke.(290) (EG 1) In a study of 215 consecutive patients with transient ischemic attack, CT scan revealed possible acute infarction in 7 of 154 patients, while MRI confirmed acute infarction in only 2 of these 7 patients. MRI also detected an acute infarct in 32.5% of the 154 patients with an initially normal baseline CT scan. The authors concluded that acute MRI, when available, is more advantageous than CT scan to investigate the etiology of lesions in patients with transient ischemic attack.(291) (EG 2) In reviews of the complementary roles of CT scan and MRI in acute stroke, the incremental benefit of CT scan over MRI included wider availability and more accurate detection of the presence of hemorrhage, while the incremental benefit of MRI over CT scan included greater sensitivity in detecting acute infarction and better ability to differentiate true stroke from stroke mimics.(292)(293)(294)(295)(296) (EG 2) Studies continue to assess the potential role of MRI for prognostication in the setting of stroke.(297)(298) (EG 2) Initial studies with MRI permeability imaging suggest, but have not confirmed, that MRI may predict hemorrhagic transformation after recanalization in the setting of acute ischemic stroke.(299) (EG 2)

For syncope, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Common etiologies include vasovagal response or orthostatic hypotension; MRI is nondiagnostic in most cases, unless syncope is associated with newly developed or focal seizures, focal neurologic findings, or episodes of syncope following head trauma.(303) (EG 2)

For trauma, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) Review articles indicate that CT scan is highly accurate for detecting injuries requiring emergency neurosurgery, such as hemorrhage or herniation; MRI is not usually indicated for the initial evaluation of traumatic brain injury because it takes longer to acquire, is generally less available, and requires additional safety precautions. However, MRI is more sensitive than CT scan to pathologic changes associated with mild traumatic brain injury and may also provide useful prognostic information.(307)(308)(309)(311) (EG 2) Appropriate-use criteria recommend brain MRI as the initial evaluation in nonemergent settings for any child with neurologic signs or symptoms, apnea, a complex skull fracture, other fractures, or injuries highly suspicious for nonaccidental head trauma.(312)(313) (EG 2)

Inconclusive or Non-Supportive Evidence

For bipolar disorder, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A meta-analysis of CT scan and MRI for this condition found robust evidence that changes in lateral ventricular size and white and gray matter characteristics were correlated with disease, but it concluded that substantial false-positive and false-negative errors remain, and further research is required.(18) (EG 2)

For psychosis without other neurologic findings (first episode), evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A retrospective review of 112 consecutive MRI and 204 consecutive CT scan studies in patients with a first episode of psychosis, but without other neurologic signs, found that 3% had brain lesions potentially contributing to psychosis on MRI, including brain tumor and HIV encephalopathy, and another 1.5% had brain lesions on CT scan; the authors indicated that routine MRI or CT scan imaging was unlikely to contribute to a significant change in management, but further prospective studies would be helpful.(19) (EG 2)
