Background

Return to top of Myocardial Perfusion Imaging, Pharmacologic Stress - AC

Pharmacologic stress myocardial perfusion imaging, such as pharmacologic stress SPECT, involves intravenous injection of a radioactive tracer (eg, thallium, sestamibi, or tetrofosmin), which is taken up by myocardial cells and visualized by a digital gamma camera, thereby reflecting the distribution of blood perfusion throughout the myocardium.(1)(2)(3) (EG 2) Coronary hyperemia is induced by a vasodilator, such as adenosine, dipyridamole, or regadenoson,(4)(5)(6) or an adrenergic agent such as dobutamine, in lieu of stress via exercise or in addition to submaximal exercise.(1)(3)(7)(8) (EG 2) A defect in the image with stress that is not present at rest usually indicates an area of myocardial ischemia.(3) (EG 2) Myocardial perfusion imaging synchronized with ECG (eg, gated SPECT) can assess ventricular function, including ejection fraction, in addition to myocardial perfusion.(2)(8) (EG 2) Pharmacologic stress testing using the vasodilator agents adenosine and dipyridamole is contraindicated in patients with severe reactive airway disease (eg, asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) because of provocation of bronchospasm; regadenoson or dobutamine may be substituted in this population.(8)(9)(10) (EG 2)

Myocardial perfusion imaging has been noted by specialty societies to have the most clinical utility in patients who are at intermediate risk for coronary artery disease, in those requiring management or prognostic information, and in those with unexplained and persistent symptoms.(11)(12)(13) (EG 2) Myocardial perfusion imaging systems that combine SPECT and CT technology (also known as "hybrid" systems) are now widely available.(8)(14)(15)(16) (EG 2) It has been noted that myocardial perfusion scans contribute at least 20% of the estimated annual collective radiation dose in the United States, although the lifetime cancer risk from a single myocardial perfusion imaging study is thought to be small.(17)(18) (EG 2) Best-practice methods to maximize diagnostic quality while minimizing radiation exposure have been proposed.(8)(19)(20)(21) (EG 2)

The evidence for the clinical indications found in this guideline includes 68 published peer reviewed articles, 38 specialty society or other evidence-based guidelines, and 1 book section.

For acute chest pain or acute anginal equivalent, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) Myocardial perfusion stress and rest imaging is useful for diagnosing ischemia in low-risk to intermediate-risk patients who have a nondiagnostic ECG and negative cardiac biomarkers.(25)(26)(33) (EG 2) Myocardial perfusion imaging is useful in patients with chest pain for whom exercise treadmill testing would be unreliable, such as those with complete left bundle branch block or an electronically paced rhythm.(26) (EG 2) A meta-analysis determined that myocardial perfusion imaging and stress echocardiography have similar high negative predictive values for myocardial infarction and cardiac death; the meta-analysis concluded that by accurately identifying patients as low risk, additional interventions such as cardiac catheterization can be avoided.(34) (EG 1) A systematic review and meta-analysis of 77 studies involving a total of 49,541 patients presenting to the emergency department with chest pain concluded that myocardial perfusion scintigraphy had a sensitivity of 85% and specificity of 92% for the detection of acute coronary syndrome.(29) (EG 1)

For asymptomatic patients, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) An appropriate-use criteria statement endorsed by multiple specialty societies notes that pharmacologic myocardial perfusion stress imaging is appropriate in asymptomatic patients who are at high risk for coronary heart disease.(25) (EG 2)

For coronary artery disease (known), evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) Myocardial perfusion imaging is useful for detecting the presence of new or worsening ischemia suggestive of myocardium-at-risk and identifying the need for changes to treatment plans, including revascularization; it is also used to detect restenosis or new areas of stenosis following revascularization.(10)(25)(33)(53) (EG 2) A meta-analysis of 166 studies found that, among myocardial perfusion imaging techniques, stress SPECT is widely available and the most extensively validated.(57) (EG 2) A prospective study of 266 patients with known coronary artery disease found that a normal myocardial perfusion imaging scan was predictive of a favorable long-term prognosis.(58) (EG 2) Pharmacologic myocardial perfusion imaging is useful for localizing cardiac ischemia in patients who are unable to exercise or cannot achieve target heart rates (usually 85% of predicted maximum heart rate on a standard Bruce protocol) with exercise stress testing, and in patients with an ECG that is not interpretable for ischemia, such as those with a complete left bundle branch block.(25) (EG 2) A study of 1126 generally asymptomatic patients without previous cardiovascular disease who had both a cardiac CT scan with a standard Agatston criteria score and a myocardial perfusion stress imaging scan by SPECT showed that 31% of patients with a score greater than 400 had an abnormal SPECT result.(59) (EG 2) Review articles and specialty society guidelines note that a coronary artery calcium score of 100 or more Agatston units indicates a high plaque burden with moderately to severely increased cardiovascular risk.(13)(60)(61) (EG 2) A review article and an appropriate-use criteria document note that myocardial perfusion stress imaging is appropriate to detect ischemia and evaluate function in patients with an anomalous aortic origin of a coronary artery.(51)(62) (EG 2)

For coronary artery disease (suspected), evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) Appropriate-use criteria statements endorsed by multiple specialty societies note that pharmacologic myocardial perfusion stress imaging is appropriate for evaluation of symptomatic patients with intermediate pretest probability who are suspected of having coronary artery disease.(25)(26) (EG 2) A systematic comparative effectiveness review and meta-analysis of 30 studies found that myocardial perfusion stress imaging has a summary sensitivity of 81% and specificity of 78% for the detection of coronary artery disease in women.(69) (EG 2) A retrospective study of 473 patients with limited exercise capacity and suspected coronary artery disease found that dobutamine stress SPECT imaging provided incremental prognostic value in addition to clinical and stress test parameters.(70) (EG 2) A specialty society consensus statement and a review article note that alternative diagnostic testing without radiation exposure should be considered for premenopausal women with suspected ischemic heart disease.(38)(71) (EG 2) A review article notes that certain congenital heart conditions are associated with risk factors for ischemic heart disease (eg, persistent hypertension in patients with repaired aortic coarctation); coronary artery compression syndromes and coronary fistulas can cause coronary ischemia, as can congenital heart surgeries that involve coronary reimplantation. Pharmacologic myocardial perfusion stress imaging can detect ischemic heart disease in these patients.(72) (EG 2) Specialty society guidelines recommend periodic noninvasive ischemia provocation testing, which may include pharmacologic myocardial perfusion stress imaging in combination with echocardiography, for adult patients who have undergone an arterial switch repair of transposition of the great vessels. These guidelines also note that stress imaging can be useful for the evaluation of myocardial ischemia in adult patients with unrepaired congenital heart disease.(62)(73) (EG 2)

For diabetes mellitus, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) There is no demonstrated clinical benefit to routine screening of asymptomatic diabetic patients, but candidates for cardiac testing include those with typical or atypical cardiac symptoms, signs or symptoms of vascular disease, or an abnormal resting ECG.(84)(86) (EG 2) Appropriate-use criteria endorsed by multiple specialty societies note that the presence of diabetes contributes to a higher pretest likelihood of coronary artery disease, making the use of noninvasive imaging such as pharmacologic myocardial perfusion stress imaging reasonable as an initial diagnostic test in patients who are suspected to have ischemic heart disease.(26) (EG 2) A study of 319 type 2 diabetic patients with stable mild anginal symptoms who underwent myocardial perfusion stress imaging by SPECT showed that those with severe demonstrated myocardial ischemia are at 3 to 6 times higher risk of cardiac death and nonfatal myocardial infarction than those with no or moderate myocardial ischemia, suggesting a more aggressive and invasive approach for those with severe ischemia.(87) (EG 2)

For heart failure, cardiomyopathy, or ventricular dysfunction, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Myocardial perfusion stress imaging is more sensitive but less specific than dobutamine stress echocardiography in predicting ventricular function improvement after revascularization (eg, percutaneous coronary intervention).(91) (EG 2) Specialty society guidelines state that myocardial perfusion stress imaging may be useful in assessing ischemia and myocardial viability in patients with heart failure.(92)(93) (EG 2)

For nonacute chest pain or ischemic equivalent, evidence demonstrates at least moderate certainty of at least moderate net benefit. (RG A1) Appropriate-use criteria statements endorsed by multiple specialty societies note that pharmacologic myocardial perfusion stress imaging is appropriate for evaluation of symptomatic patients with intermediate pretest probability who are suspected of having coronary artery disease.(25)(26) (EG 2) A retrospective cohort study of 1,491,642 patients with nonacute chest pain compared outcomes between patients who underwent noninvasive diagnostic testing (exercise treadmill test, myocardial perfusion scan, stress echocardiography, coronary CT scan) with patients who did not have diagnostic testing and found, at a median follow-up of 4.1 years, that patients who underwent noninvasive testing had a 25% lower rate of a composite outcome of cardiovascular mortality, acute myocardial infarction, or unstable angina compared with patients who had no cardiac testing.(102) (EG 2)

For preoperative cardiovascular evaluation, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) Myocardial perfusion imaging may be indicated for those circumstances in which the test results will clearly affect management of high-risk patients (eg, cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, diabetes, chronic kidney disease) prior to either cardiovascular surgery or noncardiac intermediate-risk surgeries; however, myocardial perfusion has not supplanted coronary angiography in the preoperative evaluation of possible ischemia in patients prior to surgery for aortic stenosis.(107)(108) (EG 2)

For syncope, evidence demonstrates a net benefit, but of less than moderate certainty, and may consist of a consensus opinion of experts, case studies, and common standard care. (RG A2) An appropriate-use criteria statement endorsed by multiple specialty societies notes that pharmacologic myocardial perfusion stress imaging is appropriate for the evaluation of syncope in patients who are at intermediate or high risk for coronary heart disease.(25) (EG 2)

Inconclusive or Non-Supportive Evidence

Return to top of Myocardial Perfusion Imaging, Pharmacologic Stress - AC

For follow-up of normal stress SPECT studies, evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or poor and demonstrates an incomplete assessment of net benefit vs harm; additional research is recommended. (RG B) A study investigating the timing and utility of routine follow-up stress SPECT scans found that these follow-up scans have minimal impact on referral to revascularization.(22) (EG 2)

For routine screening for coronary artery disease, evidence demonstrates a lack of net benefit; additional research is recommended. (RG C1) Specialty society guidelines note that the use of SPECT imaging is inappropriate for asymptomatic patients at low risk for coronary artery disease.(23)(24)(25)(26) (EG 2) In addition, for all patients, a specialty society statement recommends that serial myocardial perfusion imaging testing be avoided.(11) (EG 2)
